# Is there a risk to livestock?

## Short Answer

Yes, livestock can be at risk if they enter the active treatment area, touch electrodes, contact conductive vegetation or metal objects connected to the treatment path, or stand in an area affected by an unintended electrical path. Livestock should be kept outside the required safety zone whenever Zasso equipment is operating. If livestock enter the work area, the operator should stop high-voltage output and follow the product-specific procedure before restarting.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso electrical weeding uses controlled high-voltage energy to treat target plants through applicators or electrodes and the intended plant-soil pathway. Livestock are not intended to be part of that pathway. The relevant risk is not only direct contact with a visible electrode; it can also include contact with conductive plants, wet ground, puddles, fences, irrigation hardware, metal gates, trailers, or machine parts that become part of an unintended electrical path during operation or an abnormal condition.

Livestock create a particular site-control challenge because they move as individuals or groups, may be curious, may be difficult to redirect quickly, and may contact the ground and nearby objects in ways that differ from people. Cattle, horses, sheep, goats, and other farm animals may also react unpredictably to noise, movement, fencing, handlers, vehicles, or a sudden change in the work area. For that reason, the safe operating assumption is that livestock must be excluded from the active treatment area before high voltage is enabled.

For pasture, orchard, vineyard, roadside, or farmyard work, customers should plan access control before the machine starts. Gates, temporary fencing, livestock movement, handlers, spotters, signage, and coordination with the farm team may be needed. If livestock cannot be reliably kept away from the applicators, treated vegetation, conductive structures, wet areas, or the machine, the work should be paused or rescheduled until safe site control is possible.

This does not mean Zasso equipment is unsuitable for farms or areas where livestock may normally be present. It means livestock should be treated as protected bystanders during active operation, and the approved product manual, training, safety distance, emergency-stop procedure, and local rules always take priority over general FAQ language.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should check animal access as part of job planning. Before application, they should ask whether livestock can reach the treatment area through an open gate, broken fence, adjacent paddock, laneway, water point, shade area, feeding area, public path, or farm traffic route.

If livestock are nearby, the operator should not rely on the animals staying away by themselves. The practical control is to keep them outside the approved safety zone and to stop high-voltage output if they approach. This is especially important where animals are behind electric fencing, near metal gates or rails, on wet soil, close to irrigation equipment, or moving between paddocks.

For contractors and distributors, the sales message should be clear: Zasso is a professional high-voltage weed-control system, so animal exclusion is part of safe worksite management. The exact distance, signage, barrier, restart, and emergency requirements are product-specific and should come from the approved manual and training.

## Evidence and Context

Zasso's public technology material describes electrical weeding as controlled energy delivery through applicators into plants and the plant-soil pathway. That supports explaining livestock safety around preventing unintended contact with energized applicators, conductive vegetation, soil, metal, or water paths: https://zasso.com/technology/

Existing Zasso FAQ files in the GitHub repository, especially FAQ-134, FAQ-141, FAQ-143, and FAQ-144, consistently frame people, animals, and pets as needing to stay outside the active treatment area during high-voltage operation. The livestock answer follows the same conservative safety logic, with added attention to herd movement, gates, fencing, wet ground, and farm infrastructure.

Zasso internal safety and operational materials reviewed for this FAQ state that the operator must keep the risk area free of people and animals during operation, stop the equipment in an emergency or if the safety boundary is breached, and follow product-specific safety distances and procedures. Those internal sources support the high-level customer wording here, while product-specific distances, thresholds, and rescue or restart steps should remain in approved manuals and training documents.

Recent Read.AI meeting context reviewed for this FAQ reinforces that Zasso treats training, operation according to manuals, service readiness, and exclusions for misuse or unauthorized operation as important deployment topics. That supports describing livestock safety as a combination of equipment design, trained operation, site control, and manual-based procedures.

Michigan State University Extension describes electrical weed control as using charged electrodes and high-voltage electricity to treat weeds, with operation influenced by voltage, current, speed, soil moisture, plant condition, and electrode contact: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

GROW IWM's weed-electrocution guidance states that electrical weed-control equipment operates at high voltage and that improper handling can create severe shock risks for operators or nearby individuals. It recommends training, safe distances, inspection, maintenance, and caution around wet or rainy conditions: https://growiwm.org/weed-electrocution/ and https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

General farm electrical-safety guidance on stray voltage supports the principle that animals can be affected when they contact two surfaces with a voltage difference or become part of a conductive path. While stray-voltage guidance is not specific to Zasso equipment, it reinforces the conservative recommendation to prevent livestock from contacting energized, faulted, wet, or conductive paths: https://www.alliantenergy.com/safety/farm-safety/stray-voltage

## Safe Sales Wording

"Livestock should be kept outside the active treatment area whenever Zasso equipment is operating. If animals enter the safety zone, the operator should stop high-voltage output and follow the approved procedure before restarting."

"Zasso equipment is designed for trained professional operation. On farms with livestock, safe use depends on planning the worksite so animals cannot approach the applicators, treated vegetation, metal structures, wet areas, or the machine during active operation."

"Exact safety distances and restart rules are product-specific, so customers should follow the Zasso manual, training, and local requirements for their machine and application."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso equipment is harmless to livestock, safe for livestock to approach, safe in occupied pasture, or safe simply because the treatment targets plants rather than animals.

Do not provide veterinary advice, animal-rescue instructions, product-specific safety distances, discharge times, PPE rules, sensor thresholds, certification claims, or emergency procedures unless they come from approved customer-facing product documentation.

Livestock risk can vary by species and behavior, herd size, fencing, gates, handlers, terrain, soil moisture, rain, irrigation, puddles, metal structures, nearby water points, machine condition, applicator type, local regulation, and operator behavior. Customers should rely on approved manuals, training, site controls, and local Zasso support for exact requirements.
