# Does Zasso work in potatoes?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can be relevant in potato systems, but the strongest claim is application-specific rather than universal. Potatoes are a plausible fit for directed weed control before canopy closure, targeted cleanup, low-residue or organic programs, and potato haulm/desiccation-adjacent work where the crop, ridges, tubers, and irrigation can be protected. Zasso should not be presented as a blanket in-crop potato solution unless the equipment setup, timing, crop stage, and local protocol have been validated.

## Detailed Answer

Potatoes are a high-value row crop with a narrow weed-control window. Early-season weeds can reduce yield and quality, and later weeds can interfere with hilling, irrigation, harvest, tuber quality, and seed return. This makes potatoes commercially interesting for non-chemical and low-residue tools, especially in organic, seed, processor-linked, or herbicide-constrained systems.

Zasso's Electroherb technology controls contacted vegetation through electrical energy. It is a physical, non-chemical mode of action, but it is not crop-selective in the way a labelled selective herbicide may be. If the electrodes contact potato vines or other crop tissue, crop injury is possible. For potatoes, the practical question is therefore not simply "does it work?" but "which potato job is being treated, and can the target vegetation be contacted while protecting the crop and tuber quality?"

The most credible potato use cases are targeted and staged. Before crop emergence, or before the canopy closes, electrical treatment may be considered for emerged weeds in directed areas where the crop is absent or can be avoided. In inter-row or ridge-adjacent work, the applicator geometry, guidance, row spacing, soil moisture, residue, vine size, and safety protocol matter. For escape weeds or field-edge cleanup, Zasso may add value when herbicide, cultivation, or hand labor is costly, constrained, or strategically unattractive.

Potato haulm desiccation is an important adjacent use case. Historical public Zasso-linked work in Germany evaluated high-voltage electrical haulm killing in potatoes, and reported early functionality for reducing potato haulm while continuing to study tuber quality, variety, and soil-moisture effects. That supports the technical relevance of electrical treatment in potatoes, but it should not be converted into a broad promise that every potato weed-control or desiccation program can be replaced by Zasso without local validation.

## What This Means for Customers

For potato growers, Zasso should be discussed as a potential tool inside integrated weed management, not as a one-size-fits-all replacement for herbicides, hilling, cultivation, vine killing, or hand weeding. The best customer conversations start with the specific job to be improved: early inter-row weeds, organic hand-weeding cost, residue-sensitive production, escape cleanup, processor sustainability requirements, seed-field hygiene, or a desiccation-adjacent trial.

A good potato evaluation should measure more than weed control. It should check crop injury, ridge integrity, exposed or green tubers, tuber size distribution, harvest interference, irrigation risk, regrowth, speed, energy use, labor, and cost per treated hectare or acre. In many conventional potato systems, the incumbent herbicide and hilling program may still be economical. Zasso is most compelling where the avoided operation has high cost, high risk, regulatory pressure, residue concern, labor scarcity, or clear sustainability value.

Customers should expect a structured pilot before scale-up. Potato systems are sensitive to timing, variety, ridge shape, soil moisture, vine growth, and harvest-quality requirements, so the commercial promise should be tied to local proof.

## Evidence and Context

GitHub repository research reviewed existing Zasso crop-specific FAQ answers for soybeans, cotton, and sugar beet, along with internal Zasso market material on potato growers and diquat. That material consistently frames potatoes as a promising but validation-dependent segment: early weeds, hand cleanup, low-residue programs, organic production, processor requirements, and haulm/desiccation constraints can create a real need, while direct current Zasso commercial potato proof remains an evidence gap.

SharePoint search during this run returned investor and presentation material but did not surface a stronger potato-specific approved sales claim. Read.AI meeting summaries reviewed during this run did not provide a potato-specific approved claim for customer use. Those sources therefore support a conservative answer rather than a stronger commercial guarantee.

Zasso's public technology page describes Electroherb as a physical electrical mode of action in which current passes through contacted plants and the soil path, with no chemical residue left in the soil: https://zasso.com/technology/

PotatoPro reported historical Zasso-linked work on high-voltage electrical potato haulm killing in the ElektroSiKa project with the Lower Saxony Chamber of Agriculture: https://www.potatopro.com/news/2018/zasso-high-voltage-electrical-haulm-killing-be-demonstrated-potato-europe-2018

A related public release on the ElektroSiKa work reported first randomized plot-trial results showing basic functionality for reducing potato haulm and noted that further work would focus on tuber quality, variety, and soil-moisture effects: https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/innovative-physical-potato-haulm-desiccation-with-high-voltage-electricity-300615253.html

UC IPM notes that early-season weeds can reduce potato yields if not controlled within roughly 4 to 6 weeks after potato emergence, and recommends integrated weed management based on weed species, crop rotation, cultivation, herbicides, and potato competitiveness: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/potato/integrated-weed-management/

New Mexico State University similarly describes potatoes as having a critical weed-removal period of about 4 to 6 weeks after planting, with later weeds potentially interfering with harvest even when yield effects are smaller: https://pubs.nmsu.edu/research/horticulture/RR723/index.html

Michigan State University Extension describes electrical weed control as an emerging non-chemical method for escaped and herbicide-resistant weeds, while emphasizing that electrical systems are non-selective and must avoid crop contact: https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/electrical-weed-control-in-vegetables-and-field-crops

GROW IWM's weed-electrocution factsheet describes electrical weed control as non-selective and non-chemical, with performance influenced by weed density, species, and plant and soil moisture, and frames it as a supplemental integrated-weed-management tactic rather than a standalone solution: https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso can be relevant in potatoes where the application is designed around the crop stage, ridge geometry, target weeds, and crop-contact avoidance."

"For potatoes, the strongest fit is a validated, targeted use case: early directed weed control, organic or low-residue cleanup, escape management, or haulm/desiccation-adjacent trials where performance and tuber quality are measured."

"We recommend treating potato opportunities as structured pilots first, with clear measurements for weed control, crop safety, harvest quality, and operating cost."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso is selective to potato plants. Direct contact with potato vines or crop tissue can create crop-injury risk unless a specific protocol has been validated.

Do not claim universal potato efficacy, complete herbicide replacement, guaranteed tuber quality, one-pass control, season-long weed control, or regulatory acceptance for desiccation. Potato performance depends on timing, variety, row and ridge geometry, vine growth, weed species and stage, plant and soil moisture, soil conductivity, residue, speed, applicator design, irrigation layout, operator training, and local safety requirements.

Do not disclose confidential product-roadmap details, patent strategy, unpublished field data, customer names, private trial material, pricing, or non-public commercial timelines. Treat potato haulm desiccation as a promising adjacent topic unless current local evidence and approved protocols support stronger claims.
