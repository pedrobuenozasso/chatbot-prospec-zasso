# Does Zasso require re-entry intervals?

## Short Answer

Generally, not in the pesticide-label sense. Zasso's electrical treatment does not apply a herbicide active ingredient, so the treatment itself does not create a pesticide-style restricted-entry interval tied to chemical residues from that pass. That does not mean people can enter casually at any moment: access still has to be controlled during operation and until the equipment has been shut down, de-energized, and verified safe according to the approved Zasso procedure.

## Detailed Answer

When customers ask about re-entry intervals, they usually mean the restricted-entry interval, or REI, used in pesticide regulation. An REI is the period after a pesticide application when workers must stay out of the treated area to avoid exposure to pesticide residues on plants, soil, water, or in the air. That kind of restriction is tied to the use of a pesticide product and its label requirements.

Zasso changes that logic because the weed-control action is physical rather than chemical. The equipment applies controlled electrical energy through contacted vegetation and the plant-soil circuit instead of spraying or depositing a herbicide active ingredient. Because no herbicide is applied in the Zasso treatment itself, the treatment does not create a herbicide-label REI from that pass in the normal sense.

That is the important commercial point, but it needs a second safety point beside it. "No pesticide REI" is not the same as "no access control." During active operation, people must remain outside the required safety zone. After shutdown, electrodes and related components must still be treated according to the product-specific de-energizing, discharge, and verification procedure before anyone approaches for inspection, cleaning, or maintenance. In other words, Zasso generally avoids the chemical re-entry restriction associated with a sprayed herbicide pass, but it still requires electrical and machine-safety discipline.

The answer is also conditional in mixed programs. If herbicides are still used elsewhere in the customer's weed-control program, those separate pesticide applications may still carry their own restricted-entry intervals, early-entry rules, worker-protection obligations, or customer-specific access restrictions. Local rules, crop protocols, labor policies, buyer standards, and site-specific safety procedures can also affect how and when people are allowed back into the area.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, the practical benefit is that replacing a herbicide pass with Zasso can remove the pesticide-residue waiting period associated with that avoided spray application. That may make labor planning easier in orchards, vineyards, berries, public-facing sites, organic systems, or other operations where re-entry timing affects mowing, scouting, pruning, harvest support, irrigation work, or general field access.

At the same time, operators should not market or manage the treatment as if no restrictions apply at all. The right field message is that Zasso generally avoids pesticide-style re-entry intervals, while still requiring controlled access during operation, trained shutdown procedures, and product-specific confirmation that the machine is safe before close approach or contact.

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso removes every access restriction or that people can freely approach operating equipment. The defensible claim is narrower: the Zasso treatment itself generally does not create a pesticide-label re-entry interval.

Do not use this FAQ as legal or regulatory advice for every jurisdiction. Worker-protection rules, customer requirements, crop-specific standards, public-area controls, and local safety procedures can vary.

Do not extend this answer to herbicide applications that remain elsewhere in the customer's program. Those applications may still have restricted-entry intervals, early-entry rules, or other pesticide-handling obligations.

Do not confuse the absence of a chemical REI with the absence of electrical risk. During operation and shutdown, operators must still follow product-specific safety zones, de-energizing procedures, and access controls.
