# Does the current pass through the plant, the soil, or both?

## Short Answer

Both. In electrical weeding, the target plant and the surrounding soil are part of the electrical circuit. Current enters through the contacted vegetation, can move through stems, crowns, roots, and nearby soil, and returns through another applicator, contacted plant, or ground/soil return path depending on the machine and field setup. The useful weed-control effect depends on how much of that current and energy passes through the target plant tissues rather than bypassing the plant through easier soil or surface paths.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding needs a closed circuit. The applicator or electrode contacts the weed above ground, electrical energy enters the plant, and the return path is completed through the plant-soil system and a second applicator or ground-contact element. That means the answer is not "plant only" or "soil only." The plant, roots, soil, and electrode geometry work together as a variable electrical load.

The plant matters because living tissue contains water and dissolved minerals, so it can conduct current. At the same time, plant tissue has electrical resistance, which helps convert part of the delivered energy into heat and cellular damage. This is why current through stems, crowns, vascular tissue, and roots can contribute to wilting, desiccation, and regrowth suppression when dose and conditions are suitable.

The soil matters because it helps complete the return path and strongly affects how current is distributed. Soil moisture, salinity, texture, compaction, root contact, residue, electrode spacing, and whether the electrode touches bare soil can all change the path of least resistance. If the current passes mainly through the target plant and its root zone, the treatment is more likely to create useful plant injury. If too much current bypasses the plant through wet soil, bare-ground contact, or parallel vegetation pathways, the same machine output may produce weaker control.

For customers, the practical message is that Zasso is managing a plant-soil electrical circuit, not simply "shocking leaves." Good results depend on applicator contact, field conditions, travel speed, plant size and architecture, soil condition, and trained operation.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should expect electrical weeding performance to depend on both vegetation and soil conditions. A well-contacted, actively growing weed in suitable soil conditions can conduct useful energy through the plant and into recovery tissues. A poorly contacted weed, very wet soil, heavy residue, or direct electrode contact with bare ground can change the current path and reduce the energy that reaches the target plant.

This is also why operator training and field assessment matter. The machine, applicator, speed, safety setup, and operating window should be matched to the crop or site, weed spectrum, plant density, soil moisture, and service objective. Follow-up scouting remains important because visible leaf symptoms do not always prove that the plant's recovery tissues received enough energy.

## Caveats

Do not claim that all current passes through the plant, that all current reaches every root, or that the soil is only a passive ground. Current follows available conductive paths, and some energy can be lost or diverted through soil, residue, bare-ground contact, or other vegetation.

Do not promise guaranteed root kill or one-pass control. The current path and biological outcome depend on species, plant size, plant water content, root architecture, soil moisture, soil conductivity, electrode contact, operating speed, dose, and machine setup.

Do not disclose confidential machine settings, internal control logic, unpublished test data, customer-specific results, certification details, pricing, or sensitive engineering designs.
