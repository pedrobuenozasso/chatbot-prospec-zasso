# Does Zasso work on volunteer plants?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can work on volunteer plants when the plants are accessible to the electrodes and the application protocol is matched to the species, growth stage, density, moisture, and field conditions. Volunteer plants should be treated as unwanted vegetation, but not all volunteer plants respond the same way. Grasses, cereal volunteers, large plants, plants with strong regrowth reserves, or plants shielded by crop canopy or residue may need different timing, slower operation, repeat treatment, or integration with other tools.

## Detailed Answer

Volunteer plants are crop plants or cover crop plants growing where they are not wanted. From a Zasso application standpoint, they are controlled by the same core principle as weeds: contacted vegetation becomes part of the electrical pathway, and the treatment aims to damage the plant tissues that allow survival and regrowth.

This means Zasso can be relevant for volunteers such as escaped cover crops, volunteer cereals, volunteer broadleaf crops, or other unwanted crop plants, provided the plants can be safely contacted without damaging the desired crop. The best fit is usually where the volunteer plants are above the target crop, between rows, before crop emergence, in fallow or non-crop areas, or in a validated shielded/inter-row setup.

Performance depends strongly on plant biology and field setup. Young, green, accessible broadleaf volunteers may be easier to affect than dense grass volunteers or cereal volunteers with protected growing points. Large plants may be easier to contact but may also have larger reserves. Plants under canopy, hidden by residue, lodged, dry, or only partly contacted may receive less useful energy. Where volunteer plants can regrow from crowns, nodes, tillers, roots, rhizomes, tubers, or surviving growing points, follow-up scouting and repeat treatment may be needed.

For customers, the safe answer is that Zasso can be evaluated for volunteer plant control, especially when the target volunteers are clearly separated from the desired crop or when the application system is validated for the crop geometry. It should not be presented as a guaranteed one-pass solution for every volunteer species or every crop stage.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should identify the volunteer species, growth stage, density, and location before choosing Zasso as the control method. Important questions include: Are the volunteers reachable by the electrodes? Are they taller than the crop or located between rows? Is there enough green, living tissue for contact? Is residue or canopy shielding the target? Could the desired crop be contacted or included in the electrical path?

Zasso can be attractive where customers want a non-chemical tool, want to reduce herbicide reliance, are managing herbicide-resistant volunteers or weeds, need to avoid spray drift, or want a soil-disturbance-light option. It may also be relevant for cover crop termination or escaped cover crop management in suitable systems.

Because volunteer plants are often closely related to the crop system, local validation is important. The treatment plan should define acceptable crop safety, timing, speed, number of passes, regrowth monitoring, and what success means: suppression, stand reduction, termination, seed-set prevention, or preparation for another operation.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Yes, Zasso can be evaluated for volunteer plant control where the volunteer plants are accessible to the electrodes and the application is validated for the crop, species, growth stage, and field conditions."

"Volunteer plants should be treated like other unwanted vegetation: results depend on contact, plant biology, moisture, density, speed, and follow-up scouting."

"For volunteers close to a desired crop, we recommend local validation because Zasso is non-selective to contacted plants and the crop geometry matters."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso controls every volunteer plant, that one pass always terminates volunteers, or that it is safe around every crop without validation. Zasso is non-selective to plants that are contacted and included in the electrical pathway, so desired crops must be protected by timing, geometry, shielding, targeting, or application protocol.

Volunteer grasses, cereal volunteers, large plants, dense stands, lodged plants, dry plants, plants under canopy, and plants with protected growing points may be more difficult to control. Volunteers that can regrow from crowns, nodes, tillers, rhizomes, tubers, roots, or surviving growing points may require repeat passes or integration with other methods.

Avoid product-specific claims about crop safety, speed, pass number, application settings, certified uses, or regulatory status unless Zasso has approved evidence for the specific product, crop, geography, and operating protocol.
