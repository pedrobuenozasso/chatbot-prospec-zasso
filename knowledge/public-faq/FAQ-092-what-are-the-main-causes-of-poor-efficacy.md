# What are the main causes of poor efficacy?

## Short Answer

Poor efficacy usually happens when the treatment does not deliver enough useful electrical energy into the right plant tissues under the actual field conditions. The most common causes are poor electrode contact, excessive application speed, insufficient contact time, dense or shielded vegetation, difficult weed biology, unsuitable plant or soil moisture, large or mature weeds, uneven terrain, residue or mulch interference, and lack of follow-up where regrowth risk is high. Zasso performance should therefore be managed through field assessment, correct setup, trained operation, and a treatment plan matched to the weed, crop, soil, and customer objective.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding is not simply a visual burn-down process. The target plant must become part of a practical electrical circuit, and enough useful energy must reach tissues that determine recovery, such as stems, crowns, roots, rhizomes, tubers, runners, tillers, or other growing points. Poor efficacy occurs when that energy pathway is incomplete, too short, poorly directed, or spread across the wrong material.

The first major cause is contact. If electrodes only brush the top of the canopy, ride over low or prostrate weeds, miss lower stems, touch crop residue instead of green tissue, or contact bare soil instead of the target plant, less energy reaches the weed. Dense, matted, lodged, or mixed vegetation can also shield smaller weeds and split the available current across many plants.

The second cause is operating dose. If travel speed is too high for the weed and field condition, contact time may be too short. The plant may wilt or scorch visibly, but still retain enough recovery tissue to regrow. In difficult infestations, a slower pass, better applicator positioning, mowing before treatment in suitable cases, sequential applications, or integration with another tactic may be needed.

The third cause is weed biology. Species with protected growing points, large roots, crowns, rhizomes, tubers, stolons, runners, tillers, woody tissue, or high regrowth reserves are usually harder to control than small, accessible annual weeds. Grasses, perennials, vines, woody plants, and mature weeds may require a more conservative protocol and follow-up scouting.

The fourth cause is field condition. Soil moisture, plant moisture, soil conductivity, soil type, slope, residue, mulch, stubble, dry biomass, recent rain, irrigation, drought stress, and uneven ground can all change contact and current flow. Moisture is especially nuanced: some moisture can improve conductivity and grounding, while excessive moisture or easier soil pathways can dissipate energy away from the target plant. Very dry, dusty, woody, or poorly conductive conditions can also reduce treatment consistency or increase safety and fire considerations.

The fifth cause is execution. Poor efficacy can come from incorrect machine setup, worn or damaged electrodes, unsuitable applicator height or geometry, untrained operation, rushed demonstrations, poor field selection, unrealistic one-pass expectations, or evaluation too soon after treatment. A reliable program defines the target weed, growth stage, acceptable control threshold, speed, contact quality, pass strategy, safety window, and follow-up timing before the machine enters the field.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should treat weak efficacy as a diagnosis problem, not as a simple yes/no judgment on the technology. The practical question is: what prevented enough useful energy from reaching the weeds that mattered?

A good review should check weed species, growth stage, density, root or crown structure, electrode contact, operating speed, soil and plant moisture, terrain, residue, crop constraints, pass number, operator training, and evaluation timing. Once the limiting factor is identified, the treatment plan can often be improved by adjusting timing, speed, setup, mowing or residue management, pass strategy, or integration with other weed-management practices.

This is also important for sales and demonstrations. The strongest demonstrations are not the fastest or most visually dramatic; they are the ones where field conditions, weed targets, operating settings, safety rules, and evaluation timing are defined in advance. That creates realistic expectations and avoids presenting one field result as a universal guarantee.

## Safe Sales Wording

"When efficacy is below expectation, we look for the limiting factor: contact, speed, weed stage, density, moisture, soil condition, applicator setup, or follow-up strategy. Zasso works best when the treatment plan is matched to the field rather than treated as a universal one-pass recipe."

"Poor results usually mean the weeds did not receive enough useful energy in the tissues that matter for recovery. We diagnose the field conditions and adjust the protocol before drawing conclusions."

"The strongest results come from good target selection, trained operation, consistent electrode contact, suitable speed, and realistic follow-up planning."

## Caveats

Do not claim that poor efficacy is always caused by operator error or that one adjustment will fix every field. Efficacy is influenced by weed species, weed stage, root and crown biology, moisture, soil conductivity, terrain, residue, crop constraints, electrode condition, speed, contact time, pass strategy, safety requirements, and local validation.

Do not promise guaranteed control, complete root kill, zero regrowth, one-pass performance, herbicide equivalence, or a universal speed, dose, or pass number. Avoid disclosing confidential machine settings, control logic, unpublished trial data, customer-specific results, pricing, certification details, or private meeting content.
