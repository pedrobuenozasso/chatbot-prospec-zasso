# Does it harm pollinators?

## Short Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding is not designed to target pollinators, and it does not apply insecticides, herbicide active ingredients, or chemical residues that would expose bees or other pollinators after treatment. The main pollinator risk is indirect or operational: treating flowering weeds, pollinator habitat, field margins, or vegetation where insects are actively foraging could remove forage or expose insects that are directly on contacted plants. In suitable applications, risk can be managed by correct timing, targeted application, avoiding flowering areas when pollinators are active, and preserving designated habitat.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding is a physical, contact-based weed-control method. Zasso's Electroherb technology delivers electrical energy through contacted vegetation and the plant-soil circuit. It is not an insecticide and does not leave a chemical residue on flowers, pollen, nectar, soil, or water in the way a pesticide application might.

That makes the direct exposure pathway for pollinators different from chemical pest-control products. Bees, hoverflies, butterflies, moths, beetles, and other pollinators are not the target of the treatment, and they are not expected to be exposed to a persistent toxic active ingredient after the machine has passed. Available Zasso soil-fauna context and public research summaries on electric weed control are generally reassuring for soil organisms such as springtails and mites under evaluated conditions, but that evidence should not be overextended into a blanket statement for every pollinator species and every field situation.

The practical caution is that many pollinators use flowering weeds, flowering cover crops, field margins, orchard understories, vineyard alleys, hedgerows, and non-crop vegetation for nectar, pollen, shelter, or nesting resources. If those plants are treated, the technology can remove forage just as any effective vegetation-control method can. If pollinators are physically present on flowers or stems that are contacted by electrodes at the moment of treatment, direct injury cannot be ruled out.

For that reason, the responsible answer is conditional. Zasso can be part of a pollinator-conscious weed-management program when operators avoid treatment during peak pollinator activity on flowering vegetation, protect known pollinator habitat, keep desirable flowering plants outside the treatment path, and use site-specific protocols. It should not be described as automatically harmless to pollinators in every use case.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, the main benefit is that Zasso offers non-chemical vegetation control without spray drift, volatilization, pesticide residues, or post-application chemical exposure pathways. That can be valuable in orchards, vineyards, organic systems, public areas, sensitive sites, and operations trying to reduce pesticide load.

Customers should still manage the ecological function of weeds and non-crop plants. Some weeds are agronomic problems; others may provide seasonal pollen and nectar. A good protocol distinguishes high-priority target weeds from flowering resources that should be retained, delayed, mowed later, treated outside foraging periods, or protected as habitat.

In pollinator-sensitive programs, treatment planning should include timing, field scouting, flowering status, weather, time of day, field margins, cover crops, nesting areas, and local conservation goals. Where pollinator protection is a formal requirement, Zasso should be integrated into the customer's broader integrated weed management and biodiversity plan rather than treated as a standalone guarantee.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso is a non-chemical, residue-free physical weed-control technology, so it does not expose pollinators to pesticide residues after treatment. In pollinator-sensitive areas, we recommend avoiding flowering vegetation when pollinators are active and protecting designated habitat."

"The technology is designed to treat contacted vegetation, not insects. Pollinator protection comes from targeted application, timing, and habitat-aware operating protocols."

"Zasso can help reduce chemical weed-control pressure, but we should not claim that any vegetation-control method is automatically harmless to pollinators in every field situation."

## Caveats

Do not claim "no harm to pollinators" as an absolute statement. Direct injury cannot be ruled out if pollinators are on flowers or vegetation that is physically contacted during treatment.

Do not claim a biodiversity benefit solely because the method is non-chemical. Removing flowering weeds, cover crops, or field-margin vegetation can reduce forage if treatment is poorly timed or poorly targeted. Pollinator outcomes depend on the site plan, flowering stage, time of day, season, weather, field margins, surrounding habitat, nesting resources, and local conservation objectives.

Do not use soil-arthropod evidence as proof for all pollinators. Springtails and mites are useful soil-fauna indicators, but they are not honeybees, wild bees, hoverflies, butterflies, moths, or beetles visiting flowers. Pollinator-specific validation may be needed for sensitive habitats, public procurement claims, conservation programs, or regulated sites.
