# Does it change soil pH?

## Short Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding is not expected to materially change soil pH when used under validated operating conditions. The technology is a non-chemical physical treatment: it applies electrical energy through contacted weeds and the plant-soil circuit, without adding lime, acidifying inputs, salts, herbicide residues, or other soil amendments. Direct pH-specific field evidence is still limited, so the safest wording is that meaningful pH change is not expected, but customers with strict soil-health targets should verify with routine soil testing.

## Detailed Answer

Soil pH is mainly influenced by soil parent material, rainfall and leaching, fertilizer programs, liming, organic matter processes, crop removal, irrigation water, salinity, and other chemical inputs over time. Zasso's technology does not work by adding a chemical active ingredient to the soil and does not intentionally acidify, alkalize, lime, sterilize, or chemically condition the soil.

During treatment, electrical energy is delivered through the target plant and the plant-soil return path. This can affect plant tissues and roots, but the exposure is short and localized compared with practices designed to chemically change soil properties. For that reason, there is no obvious agronomic mechanism by which normal Zasso use would be expected to create a lasting field-scale shift in soil pH.

The broader soil evidence is reassuring but should not be overstated. Current Zasso materials and public studies support the view that electrical weeding can be compatible with soil-biota goals under realistic operating conditions. However, most available soil studies focus on microbial activity, soil fauna, nematodes, soil-borne pathogen indicators, weed control, or soil moisture and conductivity behavior rather than direct before-and-after pH change. That means pH claims should remain conservative until more replicated pH-specific monitoring is available across soils, crops, seasons, and repeated-use programs.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, Zasso should not be presented as a soil pH management tool. It is not a substitute for liming, acidification management, fertilizer planning, irrigation-water management, or routine agronomic soil testing.

The practical message is that Zasso can support weed control without adding chemical residues or intentionally changing soil chemistry. If soil pH is commercially important for a crop, certification program, or long-term soil-health plan, customers should keep using their normal soil-testing schedule and compare treated and untreated areas where they want local confirmation.

## Evidence and Context

Zasso's internal ecotoxicology chapter frames electrical weeding as a physical, non-chemical process and summarizes evidence showing minimal observed impact on soil fauna, soil structure, and microbial activity under realistic field doses. It does not provide strong direct pH-specific claims, so this FAQ keeps the evidence level at Low and avoids saying that pH never changes.

Existing Zasso FAQ soil answers consistently position the technology as non-chemical, residue-free, and targeted through the plant-soil electrical circuit. FAQ-210, FAQ-211, and FAQ-212 support conservative wording that realistic use has not shown broad soil harm in tested contexts, while noting that long-term and site-specific monitoring remains important.

Public evidence also supports caution. Zasso's public technology page describes Electroherb as a direct physical process that works through plant contact and leaves no residues in the soil: https://zasso.com/technology/

GROW Integrated Weed Management summarized preliminary weed-electrocution soil research and reported no observed differences in several soil-biota indicators in referenced trials, but it did not establish a broad pH-specific claim: https://growiwm.org/after-the-volts-is-weed-electrocution-safe-for-the-soil/

A 2024 Department of Primary Industries and Regional Development Western Australia project using Zasso XPower reported no evidence of damage to soil biota, including bacterial, archaeal, and fungal soil microbiome communities and free-living nematodes. This supports broader soil compatibility but is not direct proof of no soil pH change: https://library.dpird.wa.gov.au/rqmsplant/935/

A DPIRD conference paper explains that electric weed control passes current through plant foliage, roots, and soil, and that soil moisture affects electrical behavior. This is relevant context for the plant-soil circuit but does not show persistent pH alteration: https://library.dpird.wa.gov.au/conf_papers/299/

A 2023 Weed Science review describes electric weed control as promising while emphasizing that ecological and soil-related impacts still need more short-term and long-term research across soils, climates, application histories, and organisms: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/exploring-the-potential-of-electric-weed-control-a-review/231EE50C385EF8962CDC46055C264237

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso is not designed to change soil pH. It is a non-chemical physical weed-control technology, so under validated use we would not expect a meaningful pH shift, but customers should rely on routine soil testing where pH is a critical agronomic parameter."

"The responsible claim is that Zasso does not add chemical residues or soil amendments that would intentionally acidify or alkalize the soil. For formal soil-health programs, local monitoring can confirm pH trends under the customer's own conditions."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso can improve, correct, stabilize, or permanently avoid changes in soil pH. Do not claim that pH can never change after treatment, because soil pH naturally varies with sampling depth, season, moisture, fertilizer history, liming, irrigation water, organic matter, and laboratory method.

The evidence base is stronger for general soil-biota compatibility than for direct pH-specific monitoring. If a customer needs a formal answer for certification, regulatory review, or long-term soil-health reporting, recommend local baseline and follow-up soil tests using consistent sampling depth, timing, lab method, and treated-versus-untreated comparisons.
