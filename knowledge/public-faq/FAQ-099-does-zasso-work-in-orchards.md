# Does Zasso work in orchards?

## Short Answer

Yes. Zasso can work in orchards when the weeds are emerged, reachable by the electrodes, and the machine configuration fits the crop geometry. The best fit is usually tree-row or under-tree weed control in perennial crops such as apple, citrus, nut, and similar organized orchard systems, where customers want to reduce reliance on herbicides or soil-disturbing alternatives. Performance should always be validated locally because orchard spacing, trunks, roots, irrigation, canopy, soil moisture, weed stage, and dry biomass can all affect results.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding technology is a contact-based, non-chemical method for controlling unwanted vegetation. In orchards, this means it is most relevant where the applicator can reach the weed strip or under-tree zone while avoiding crop contact and sensitive infrastructure.

Available Zasso materials support orchard use as a real application area, not just a theoretical idea. Zasso's public XPS orchard/vineyard positioning describes movable electrodes intended to treat weeds near vines and fruit trees while respecting the stem. Zasso also published preliminary apple-orchard results from CTIFL in France, where an XPS system was tested in 2020 against a chemical reference. Those preliminary tests reported broadly similar weed-cover efficiency to the chemical reference under the tested conditions and also identified the need for orchard-adapted applicator geometry, including length and height adjustment for mounds between fruit trees.

For customers, the practical answer is: yes, Zasso can be a strong orchard tool in suitable conditions, especially as part of integrated orchard-floor management. It should not be presented as a universal one-pass replacement for every orchard herbicide, mower, cultivator, hand-weeding, or flame-weeding program. Orchard applications must be designed around the crop, weed pressure, soil and moisture conditions, tree age, row spacing, canopy clearance, irrigation equipment, fire risk, and operator training.

## What This Means for Customers

Orchard customers should look at Zasso as a potential way to reduce chemical reliance, manage tree-row weeds without soil disturbance, and add a physical weed-control tool to the orchard program. It can be especially relevant for growers under residue, organic, sustainability, resistance, labor, or regulatory pressure.

The strongest use cases are organized orchards with repeatable rows, sufficient access, visible weed bands, trained operators, and a clear baseline for comparison. A good pilot should compare Zasso against the current program, such as herbicide strips, mowing, cultivation, hand cleanup, organic contact herbicides, flame, or combinations of these.

The key customer expectation is local validation. Zasso should be tested on representative blocks and evaluated for weed control, regrowth, crop safety, irrigation safety, operating speed, fuel or energy use, fire risk, and cost per treated hectare or treated strip.

Zasso's public apple-orchard release describes preliminary 2020 CTIFL testing in France using the XPS system in an apple orchard. In that tested setting, Electroherb and the chemical reference had broadly similar soil-cover efficiency by the end of the season, and the release notes that orchard-compatible applicator adaptations were identified: https://zasso.com/positive-xps_apple_tests/

Internal Zasso repository materials reviewed for this run support orchard and perennial-crop positioning, including under-canopy logic, citrus/orange orchard target-customer notes, and tree-nut orchard target-customer notes. These materials consistently frame orchard use as promising but dependent on crop geometry, contact quality, crop safety, irrigation layout, fire-risk controls, and measured local economics.

SharePoint research found a Zasso orchard press-release document consistent with the public Zasso apple-orchard release. Recent Read.AI context also supported broader current commercial interest in perennial-crop configurations such as citrus, olive, vineyard, and avocado applications, but did not provide a new approved universal orchard efficacy claim.

External orchard weed-management sources reinforce why orchards are an important application area. Washington State University notes that orchard weed strips are difficult areas where weeds compete with trees for water and nutrients and can interfere with irrigation, pests, and operations. Cornell/extension materials on electrical weed control in apple orchards report promising preliminary results but also emphasize that more work is needed and that performance depends on treatment settings, speed, and integration with other tactics.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Yes, Zasso can work in orchards where the weed strip is accessible and the applicator is matched to the orchard geometry. We validate the setup locally because crop safety, irrigation layout, weed stage, soil moisture, and operating conditions all matter."

"For orchards, Zasso is best positioned as a non-chemical tool for tree-row or under-tree weed management that can complement mowing, cultivation, reduced herbicide programs, or organic weed-control strategies."

"Preliminary orchard work and Zasso's XPS positioning support orchard use, but performance commitments should be based on a measured pilot in the customer's crop and conditions."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso works equally well in every orchard. Results depend on crop geometry, weed size, species, density, soil moisture, plant moisture, contact time, application speed, and equipment setup.

Do not claim Zasso is crop-selective if electrodes contact crop tissue. Orchard use requires careful setup to avoid trunks, low branches, roots, irrigation lines, trellis components, workers, animals, and other sensitive objects.

Do not promise full herbicide replacement, one-pass control, season-long control, or lower cost in every orchard. Zasso should be compared against the customer's full current weed-management program and validated locally.

Dry biomass, wind, drought conditions, mulch, straw, and residue can increase operational and fire-risk concerns. Local safety procedures and no-go conditions should be followed.
