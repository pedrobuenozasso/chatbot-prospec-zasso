# Does rain before application affect results?

## Short Answer

Yes. Rain before application can affect Zasso results because it changes soil moisture, plant moisture, surface water, residue condition, traction, and the electrical path between the plant and soil. Light rain or moderately moist soil may still be workable in suitable conditions, but heavy rain, saturated soil, mud, standing water, or wet residue can reduce efficiency and may create safety or access limits. The right application window depends on local field assessment, trained operation, product guidance, and the target weed stage.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding is not affected by rain in the same way as a foliar herbicide, because there is no chemical spray deposit that needs to dry on the leaf. The main issue is electrical and operational: rain changes how easily current moves through the target plant, soil, water films, and surrounding field materials.

Some pre-application moisture can be useful. Green, hydrated plants can conduct current well, and moderately moist soil can help complete the plant-soil circuit. This is one reason Zasso should not tell customers that fields must always be completely dry before treatment.

However, too much rain can work against the application. Wet or saturated soil has lower resistance, so current may spread through soil, surface water, mud, or wet residue instead of being concentrated through the target plant and root zone. Heavy rain can also make the field unsafe or impractical to enter, reduce traction, increase compaction risk, interfere with stable electrode contact, hide weeds under water or residue, and trigger product-specific safety restrictions.

For customers, the practical distinction is between manageable moisture and unsuitable wet conditions. Dew, light rain before treatment, or slightly moist soil may be acceptable if the machine can operate safely and maintain good contact. Recent heavy rain, puddles, saturated soil, active rain, mud, or waterlogged conditions should be treated as a stop-and-assess situation, not as a guaranteed efficacy advantage.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should include recent rainfall in the pre-application field check. The operator should assess how much rain fell, how long ago it fell, whether the soil is only moist or actually saturated, whether there is standing water, whether weeds remain visible and contactable, whether residue is wet or dry, whether the machine can travel safely, and whether the product manual and local procedures allow operation.

If rain has only created moderate moisture and field access is good, Zasso may still be a suitable non-chemical weed-control option. In some cases, operators may adjust speed or timing to keep enough useful energy in the target vegetation. If the rain has created waterlogged, muddy, or unsafe conditions, postponing treatment is the safer and more credible recommendation.

The customer-facing message should be balanced: rain before application is an operating factor, not a simple yes/no rule. Good results come from matching the application to soil moisture, plant condition, weed stage, contact quality, travel speed, safety rules, and local agronomic validation.

## Evidence and Context

GitHub repository research reviewed adjacent Zasso FAQ answers on wet weeds, soil moisture, dry soil, residue and mulch, conductivity, impedance, the plant-soil circuit, speed, contact time, and field-condition effects. These materials consistently frame Zasso efficacy as dependent on current passing through the target plant-soil system with good contact, appropriate speed, and suitable moisture conditions.

The Zasso GitHub white paper note "Does Soil Moisture Impact Electric Weed Control Efficiency?" summarizes Western Australian DPIRD experiments using Zasso XPower / XPU equipment. The note reports that soil moisture can influence electric weed-control outcomes, but not as a universal wet-versus-dry rule. It supports recording recent rainfall or irrigation, soil moisture, weed stage, drought stress, and operating settings in field trials and customer recommendations.

SharePoint R&D bibliography material, including the DPIRD final project report "What is the best fit for electric weed control in Australia?", gives direct rain-relevant context. The report states that 2-3 mm simulated rainfall did not show a surface-moisture control penalty in the reported conditions, while 20 mm rainfall reduced mature annual ryegrass control at 2 km/h and suggested considering slower speed under high surface moisture. The same report highlights that moist-soil control can be achieved with appropriate speed, while also keeping results tied to specific conditions.

Public DPIRD material states that high soil moisture may reduce electric weed-control efficiency because wet soil has lower resistance and can allow current to dissipate out of the roots. It concludes that soil moisture should be considered during application and that wet conditions may require reducing speed: https://library.dpird.wa.gov.au/conf_papers/299/

Oregon State University Extension guidance for electrical weed control in organic blueberry fields states that slightly moist soil can provide better conductivity, while efficiency can drop if soil is too dry or too wet. It also emphasizes good electrode contact and slower speed for deeper injury: https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9716-how-speed-timing-affect-electrical-weed-control-organic-blueberry-fields

A peer-reviewed Weed Science article on electric weed control using Zasso XPower in vineyards identifies plant species, plant age, soil type, and weather conditions during application as factors that can affect efficacy, and explains that soil properties and moisture can influence current dispersal from roots into surrounding soil: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/electric-weed-controlhow-does-it-compare-to-conventional-weed-control-methods/488C45B191B97E84E34257E80EB9CFC6

GROW IWM safety guidance cautions that electrical weed control should not be used in wet or rainy conditions because moisture can increase the likelihood of accidental discharge. This source should be treated as a general safety caution; actual Zasso operation must follow the applicable product manual, training, and local risk assessment: https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

## Safe Sales Wording

"Rain before application can change results because it changes the electrical path through the plant, soil, and surface water. Slightly moist conditions may be workable, but saturated soil, mud, standing water, or active rain require a safety and field-condition check before treatment."

"We do not treat rain as automatically good or bad. We assess recent rainfall, soil moisture, weed stage, contact quality, speed, field access, and safety procedures before recommending the application window."

"If rainfall has made the field waterlogged or unsafe, the best recommendation is to wait for a more controllable application window."

## Caveats

Do not claim that rain before application always improves results or always prevents treatment. Moisture can improve conductivity in some situations, but excessive moisture can dissipate energy away from the target plant or create safety and operating limits.

Do not recommend operation during active rain, standing water, saturated soil, mud, or unsafe field-access conditions without checking the applicable Zasso product manual, local training, site procedures, and safety rules.

Do not promise one-pass control, fixed speed adjustments, or universal rainfall thresholds. The effect of rain depends on soil texture, conductivity, slope, residue, plant water content, weed species, weed stage, density, electrode contact, product configuration, and local safety requirements.

Do not disclose confidential machine settings, unpublished trial data, internal safety thresholds, private customer results, pricing, certification details, or private meeting content.
