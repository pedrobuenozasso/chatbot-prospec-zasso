# Is the process thermal, electrolytic, electrical, or all of these?

## Short Answer

Zasso's process is fundamentally electrical: the machine creates a controlled circuit through the target plant and the plant-soil pathway. The main plant-damaging effect is best described as electrothermal, because electrical resistance in plant tissues converts part of the energy into heat, which disrupts cells, membranes, and vascular function. Electrochemical or electrolytic effects may occur at a local level when electricity passes through moist, ion-containing tissues and soil, but they should not be presented as the primary weed-control mechanism.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding starts with electricity, not with a chemical active ingredient. An applicator or electrode contacts the target vegetation, current enters the plant, and the circuit closes through another applicator, contacted plants, roots, soil, or another return path depending on the equipment configuration and field conditions. The plant is therefore part of an electrical circuit.

Once current moves through living plant tissue, several things happen together. Plant tissues contain water and dissolved ions, so they conduct electricity, but they also resist current flow. That resistance converts part of the electrical energy into heat inside the plant. The heat and electrical stress can damage membranes, rupture cells, injure xylem and phloem, and interrupt movement of water, nutrients, and sugars. In customer language, this is why Zasso can say the process damages the plant internally rather than simply burning the leaf surface.

For that reason, the best simple explanation is: electrical in delivery, electrothermal in the main lethal effect, and biological in the final plant response. The electricity is the input. Resistive heating and electrical stress are the immediate physical effects in the tissue. Wilting, desiccation, transport failure, and possible plant death are the biological outcomes when enough useful energy reaches the critical tissues.

The word electrolytic needs more care. Electrolysis means electricity driving chemical reactions in an electrolyte. Because plants and soils contain water and ions, small local electrochemical effects can occur at interfaces or in conductive fluids. However, current Zasso customer-facing materials, internal technical context, university extension material, and weed-science literature support resistive heating, cellular rupture, vascular disruption, and current-path effects as the practical mechanism to explain. Sales teams should therefore avoid saying that Zasso kills weeds by electrolysis unless Zasso has specific approved technical wording for a particular product or claim.

The distinction matters because different words create different customer expectations. Calling the process only thermal can make it sound like flame, steam, or hot water weeding, which is incomplete because electricity travels through the plant along a circuit. Calling it only electrical can be too vague. Calling it electrolytic can imply chemistry or residues that are not the core Zasso value proposition. The safest wording is that Zasso uses controlled electrical energy to create internal electrothermal and cellular damage in target plants, with results depending on the current path, dose, contact, plant structure, soil conditions, moisture, speed, and trained operation.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers can understand Zasso as a non-chemical physical mode of action. There is no herbicide active ingredient being sprayed, no chemical residue mechanism being relied on, and no claim that the process works like a systemic herbicide molecule moving through the plant. Instead, the machine uses electricity to deliver energy through the plant and its surrounding circuit.

This helps customers compare Zasso with alternatives. It is different from herbicides because it is not a biochemical active ingredient. It is different from flame or steam because energy is delivered through electrical contact and current flow rather than only from outside heat. It is different from mowing or cultivation because it aims to damage internal recovery pathways without necessarily cutting or moving soil.

For operating expectations, the important point is that the mechanism still depends on agronomy and conditions. Good electrode contact, suitable plant stage, plant water content, soil moisture, soil conductivity, application speed, machine setup, and operator training all influence where the energy goes and whether it reaches the tissues that control regrowth.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso is an electrical weed-control technology. It uses controlled electrical energy to make the plant part of a circuit, and the plant's resistance converts part of that energy into internal heat and cellular damage."

"The process is best described as electrical in delivery and mainly electrothermal in the plant effect. It is not a herbicide chemistry, and we should not describe electrolysis as the main weed-killing mechanism."

"The goal is to damage the plant's internal transport and recovery tissues, with performance depending on plant species, growth stage, contact quality, soil and moisture conditions, application speed, and trained operation."

## Caveats

Do not describe Zasso as simply burning weeds. Heat is important, but the mechanism depends on electrical contact, current path, power delivery, plant resistance, vascular disruption, and root or crown effects.

Do not describe Zasso as primarily electrolytic unless there is approved product-specific technical language. Electrochemical effects may occur locally, but the practical weed-control explanation should focus on electrical current, resistive heating, cellular rupture, and transport failure.

Do not claim instant death, universal root kill, zero environmental impact, or guaranteed one-pass control. Results depend on field conditions, plant morphology, moisture, soil conductivity, machine setup, safety procedures, and operator training.

Do not disclose confidential power settings, control algorithms, electrode designs, unpublished trial data, telemetry, customer-specific results, pricing, certification plans, or sensitive engineering details.
