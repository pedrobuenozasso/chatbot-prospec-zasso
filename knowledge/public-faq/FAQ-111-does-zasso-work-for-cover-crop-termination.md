# Does Zasso work for cover crop termination?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can be relevant for cover crop termination in suitable conditions, especially where growers want a non-chemical option before planting or a rescue tool after incomplete termination. Early university work with Zasso equipment has shown promising results on species such as oats, crimson clover, winter peas, and brown mustard, while cereal rye and hairy vetch were harder to terminate and may require multiple passes or different timing. The safest customer-facing position is that Zasso is a promising cover-crop termination tool, but performance must be validated by species, growth stage, moisture, residue, weather, fire risk, and planting system.

## Detailed Answer

Cover crop termination is the process of stopping a cover crop before it competes with the following cash crop. Growers commonly terminate cover crops with herbicides, roller-crimping, mowing, tillage, winterkill, grazing, or combinations of these methods. Zasso's electrical weeding technology may fit this space because it is a non-chemical, contact-based method for damaging green plant tissue.

The best current evidence supports a qualified yes, not a universal claim. Public GROW IWM reporting describes one-year research in Oregon and Texas using Zasso equipment for cover crop termination. In Oregon, an oat-crimson clover mixture responded well to Zasso EH30 treatment, with one pass achieving substantial termination and mowing plus electrical treatment improving termination further. In Texas, brown mustard and winter pea were reported as easier to terminate, while cereal rye and hairy vetch required more intensive treatment to reach high termination levels and some cereal rye regrowth was still observed.

This species difference matters commercially. Cover crops are not one crop; they include cereals, grasses, legumes, brassicas, broadleaf species, and multi-species mixes. A flowering mustard, an oat-clover mix, a dense cereal rye mat, and a hairy vetch stand can behave very differently under electrical treatment. Plant moisture, root architecture, growth stage, biomass, electrode contact, soil moisture, and weather can all influence whether energy is delivered effectively into the target plants.

Zasso should therefore be positioned as a potential addition to the cover-crop termination toolbox. It may be most attractive in organic no-till, reduced-herbicide, residue-sensitive, herbicide-resistant, or pre-plant systems where the grower wants to terminate green biomass without full-width tillage. It may also be useful as a rescue or second-pass tactic when herbicide, roller-crimping, mowing, grazing, or winterkill leaves live patches that could compete with the cash crop.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should evaluate Zasso for cover crop termination by defining the agronomic objective first: full termination, reduced regrowth, preserved mulch cover, reduced herbicide use, reduced tillage, organic compatibility, faster planting readiness, or rescue of escaped cover crop patches.

A good field evaluation should compare Zasso against the customer's current termination program. Important measures include termination percentage by species, regrowth after 7 to 30 days, cash-crop emergence, planter performance, mulch quality, weed suppression after planting, pass speed, energy use, number of passes, weather conditions, plant and soil moisture, dry residue, fire-risk controls, cost per hectare or acre, and whether a backup pass is still needed.

For growers, the practical message is: Zasso may offer a non-chemical termination option in selected systems, but the protocol should be matched to the cover crop species, termination timing, and local field conditions. It should not be sold as a guaranteed one-pass solution for all cover crops.

## Evidence and Context

GitHub repository research found Zasso market and white-paper notes treating cover crops as a strategically relevant, early-stage opportunity for electrical termination. Those notes frame the strongest use case as pre-plant or pre-emergence management of cover crop biomass, especially where growers want to reduce herbicide dependence or avoid soil disturbance.

SharePoint research surfaced a broad electrical-weeding technical reference that supports the general mechanism of plant electrocution, including the importance of soil moisture, plant conductivity, contact time, electrode arrangement, and mowing before electrical treatment. It did not provide a stronger approved commercial claim for cover-crop termination in this run. Recent Read.AI meeting summaries reviewed during this run did not show a specific cover-crop termination decision or approved customer-facing claim.

Public GROW IWM reporting from September 23, 2025 described early Oregon State and Texas A&M work using Zasso EH30 or EH60 equipment for cover crop termination. It reported strong preliminary results for oats, crimson clover, winter peas, and brown mustard, while noting that cereal rye and hairy vetch were harder to terminate and that only one year of research had been completed: https://growiwm.org/could-we-shock-cover-crops-for-termination/

Washington State University's Weeders of the West project page describes the EH30 Thor from Zasso applying electricity to an oats and red clover cover-crop mixture in western Oregon for termination before planting: https://smallgrains.wsu.edu/weeders-of-the-west/2025/08/14/ewc/

GROW IWM's cover-crop termination guidance explains that successful termination is essential, that cover crops can become weeds if termination fails, and that more than one termination method or pass may be needed when the first attempt fails: https://growiwm.org/cover-crop-termination-options/

USDA ERS reported that U.S. cover-crop acreage increased from 15,390,674 acres in 2017 to 17,985,831 acres in 2022, equal to 4.7% of total U.S. cropland, showing a growing but still selective market for cover-crop management: https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/108950

SARE notes that termination timing can affect soil nitrogen, cash-crop establishment, and weed control, and identifies herbicides, roller crimpers, and other termination options as part of the management decision: https://www.sare.org/resources/terminating-cover-crops-for-maximum-benefits/

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso can be evaluated as a non-chemical cover-crop termination tool, especially before planting or where the grower wants to reduce reliance on herbicide or tillage."

"Early university work with Zasso equipment is promising for some cover crop species, but species, timing, moisture, residue, and fire-risk conditions must be validated locally."

"For cover crops, we recommend a structured field comparison against the grower's current termination method, with regrowth, planting readiness, mulch quality, safety, and cost measured after treatment."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso terminates all cover crops, replaces glyphosate in every system, guarantees one-pass termination, eliminates regrowth, or works under all moisture and residue conditions.

Performance depends on cover crop species, mixture composition, growth stage, flowering or reproductive timing, plant water content, root structure, biomass, stand density, soil moisture, soil conductivity, residue load, electrode contact, speed, power delivery, weather, wind, dew, dry biomass, operator training, and local safety procedures.

Cereal rye, hairy vetch, annual ryegrass, perennial clovers, dense grass stands, dry plants, and high-residue systems require extra caution. Some species may need multiple passes, different timing, or integration with mowing, roller-crimping, grazing, herbicides, or another locally accepted termination method.

Fire risk must be managed carefully, especially in dry biomass, dry mulch, straw, drought, wind, and after mowing where residue may form a dry blanket. Public reporting on cover-crop electrocution specifically flagged plant moisture and weather as important for both efficacy and safety.
