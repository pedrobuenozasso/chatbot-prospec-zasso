# Is the safe zone because of step voltage or electrode contact risk?

## Short Answer

The safe zone is not only because of one risk. It is designed to keep people and animals away from several possible high-voltage exposure paths, including direct electrode contact, contact with conductive material touching the electrodes, step voltage in the soil, touch voltage on conductive objects, arcing, abnormal faults, and moving equipment. For customers, the practical rule is simple: follow the approved Zasso manual and training for the product-specific safety distance, and do not enter the protected zone while high voltage may be active.

## Detailed Answer

Step voltage is one reason a safety zone matters. Step voltage can occur when electrical current creates a voltage gradient across the ground or soil, so a person or animal standing with two separated contact points could bridge different potentials without touching the machine. This risk can depend on soil moisture, soil resistivity, electrode geometry, return-path design, current level, fault behavior, and how close the person or animal is to the active or faulted area.

However, the safe zone is also about electrode contact and indirect contact. A person could be exposed by touching an energized electrode, wet vegetation, a tool, a metal object, a trellis wire, irrigation hardware, a fence, or another conductive object connected to the treatment path. Touch voltage can also occur if an accessible conductive part, field object, or machine element is at a different potential from the soil or standing surface.

For that reason, Zasso should not explain the safe zone as being only about step voltage or only about touching the electrodes. It is a controlled no-entry boundary around the whole active treatment environment. It helps manage visible hazards, such as electrodes and moving machinery, as well as less visible hazards, such as conductive wet plants, soil potential gradients, transferred potential through metal objects, residual charge, faults, arcs, and site-control failures.

The exact safe zone must come from the approved product manual, training, labels, and local operating rules. Different machines, applicators, crops, field conditions, soil moisture, nearby metal, public access, and regulatory contexts can require different controls. If the correct safety distance or procedure is unclear, the conservative action is to stop operation and confirm the approved instruction before energizing the equipment.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should treat the safe zone as a strict no-entry area while high voltage may be active. Nobody should enter it to inspect weeds, move tools, touch plants, adjust equipment, cross near the applicator, approach animals, or talk to the operator during operation.

The safe zone should be planned before work begins. In a closed field, that may require operator briefing and supervision. In demonstrations, orchards, vineyards, roadsides, public areas, contractor sites, or areas with livestock or workers nearby, it may require signage, cones, barriers, spotters, traffic control, animal control, and a clear stop-work rule.

The customer benefit is clarity. Instead of debating whether the risk is step voltage or electrode contact, the team follows one practical rule: keep people, animals, tools, and conductive objects outside the product-specific protected zone, stop output if the zone is breached, and restart only when the area is controlled again.

## Caveats

Do not say that the safe zone is required only because of step voltage, or only because of direct electrode contact. Both can matter, and other hazards can matter too.

Do not present any universal Zasso safety distance in this FAQ. Product-specific distances, PPE rules, discharge times, sensor details, fault thresholds, emergency procedures, certification claims, and operating restrictions should come only from approved product documentation and training.

Do not claim that the safety zone eliminates all risk by itself. Safe operation also depends on product design, approved configuration, trained operators, inspections, grounding or return-path control, insulation, monitoring, alarms, shutdown behavior, maintenance, site control, weather and soil conditions, and local regulation.
