# Why does contact time matter?

## Short Answer

Contact time matters because electrical weeding depends on transferring enough useful energy from the electrodes into the target plant and plant-soil circuit. If contact is too brief, the plant may show visible injury but still have enough living crown, root, rhizome, or growing-point tissue to recover. Longer or repeated contact can improve control, but the right exposure depends on the weed, crop, applicator, soil condition, power delivery, safety limits, and field productivity goals.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding is not simply a question of touching a plant with electricity. The electrodes must make effective physical contact, close a usable electrical path, and maintain that path long enough for energy to damage the plant tissues that support survival and regrowth.

When contact time is adequate, more useful energy can move through the above-ground tissues and into the parts of the plant connected to the root or crown system. This increases the chance of deeper injury, vascular disruption, cell damage, and reduced regrowth. When contact time is too short, treatment may mainly scorch or injure exposed foliage, especially in large, dense, grassy, lodged, or perennial weeds. Those plants may look damaged at first but recover from protected tissues or underground reserves.

Contact time is influenced by several operating variables. Ground speed is the most obvious: slower travel usually gives electrodes more time on each target plant, while faster travel can reduce dose per plant. Electrode height, angle, pressure, shape, contact surface, weed density, weed height, canopy shielding, soil moisture, soil conductivity, crop proximity, residue, and terrain also affect how reliably the machine contacts the biological target.

For Zasso, the practical message is that contact time is part of controlled dose delivery. It must be balanced with field capacity, energy use, safety, crop protection, fire-risk management, and the planned treatment strategy. In some cases a slower pass is preferred; in others, repeated moderate-speed passes can be a better operational approach, especially where regrowth is expected.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should treat contact quality and exposure time as core parts of application quality, not as minor details. Good electrode contact can be the difference between superficial injury and a commercially useful result.

In the field, this means operators should adjust speed and applicator setup to match weed stage, weed density, plant architecture, crop clearance, soil condition, and product guidance. Dense or tall weeds may need slower travel, improved electrode positioning, mowing before treatment in suitable situations, or sequential passes. Small, upright, accessible weeds under favorable soil conditions may allow a more productive operating speed.

The customer benefit is better decision-making: contact time helps explain why the same machine can perform differently across fields, seasons, weed species, and operators. It also helps avoid unrealistic promises. A treatment plan should target enough exposure for the weed problem while still respecting productivity, safety, and economics.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Contact time matters because the electrodes need enough time to transfer useful energy into the weed, not just touch the leaves. We set speed and applicator position so the treatment matches the weed stage, density, soil condition, and desired control level."

"Good contact time helps move the treatment from superficial burn-down toward deeper injury and lower regrowth risk, but the right protocol is always product- and field-specific."

"Zasso focuses on controlled energy delivery, trained operation, and field-specific settings so contact time, power delivery, electrode contact, and safety are managed together."

## Caveats

Do not claim that longer contact time always guarantees control, that one pass always kills roots, or that a single contact-time value applies across all crops, weeds, products, soils, and seasons. Excessive slowing can reduce field capacity and may create other operational, safety, crop-proximity, energy-use, or fire-risk constraints.

Do not disclose confidential speed settings, control logic, electrode design details, customer-specific results, telemetry, certification details, pricing, or unpublished engineering parameters. Use product-specific guidance and local field validation before making performance commitments.
