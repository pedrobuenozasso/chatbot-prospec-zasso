# Why use Zasso instead of hoeing?

## Short Answer

Use Zasso instead of hoeing when the goal is to control emerged weeds without slicing, scraping, or moving the soil. In suitable applications, Zasso can provide non-chemical weed control while preserving surface cover and avoiding the repeated shallow soil disturbance that hoeing often creates. The safe commercial position is not that Zasso replaces hoeing everywhere, but that it can be a strong alternative when the customer wants weed control without a soil-cutting pass.

## Detailed Answer

Hoeing is a familiar and useful mechanical weed-control method. Depending on the tool and setup, it can cut weeds below the soil surface, uproot small seedlings, scrape the surface, or loosen soil around the row. In the right timing window, especially on small weeds, hoeing can be effective and operationally simple. Its limitation is that it works by mechanical disturbance. That means it can move soil, expose bare ground, disturb residues, clip crop roots if used too aggressively, and often requires repeat passes as new weeds emerge or partially injured weeds recover.

Zasso is chosen instead of hoeing when the customer wants a different mode of action. Electroherb is designed to transfer controlled electrical energy through contacted vegetation and the plant-soil circuit, without depending on blades, scraping, or shallow cultivation. That can make Zasso attractive where the customer wants non-chemical control with less direct soil disturbance, wants to avoid repeatedly moving soil near crop rows, or wants a treatment aimed at deeper plant injury rather than simply cutting the weed off near the surface.

This difference matters most in perennial crops, under-row management, erosion-sensitive areas, no-till or reduced-till programs, and fields where repeated hoeing may bring new weed seeds into the germination zone or disturb the crop root zone. If the customer's current hoeing pass is mainly a weed-control pass, Zasso may be a credible alternative. If the customer also relies on hoeing for crust breaking, soil loosening, or very selective in-row work around small crops, then the comparison is not one-to-one and hoeing may still have a role.

The safest customer-facing comparison is therefore practical: use hoeing when a shallow mechanical pass is the best fit for the crop stage and field condition; use Zasso when the main goal is non-chemical weed control without a hoeing pass, subject to weed species, size, density, moisture, crop proximity, equipment fit, safety setup, and local validation.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, Zasso can be valuable where weed control is needed but soil movement is undesirable. That may include orchards, vineyards, citrus, berries, olives, coffee, no-till or reduced-till systems, erosion-prone sites, and situations where repeated hoeing creates labor, regrowth, or soil-management tradeoffs.

The decision should still be made against the customer's actual hoeing job. If the weeds are very small, the crop is sensitive, and the operator needs precise shallow mechanical action close to the row, hoeing may remain the better tool. If the customer wants a non-chemical option that avoids repeated scraping or slicing of the soil surface and may provide a more durable effect on suitable weeds, Zasso may offer better value.

Customers should validate the fit under local conditions. Weed species, weed stage, root biology, soil and plant moisture, residue cover, stones, slope, crop geometry, travel speed, pass number, and traffic management all matter. In some programs, the best result may come from reducing selected hoeing passes rather than eliminating hoeing completely.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Use Zasso instead of hoeing when the customer wants non-chemical weed control without repeatedly scraping, slicing, or moving the soil."

"Zasso is often strongest where the current hoeing pass is mainly for weed control and the customer wants a lower-disturbance alternative, especially in perennial crops or erosion-sensitive systems."

"If the customer also needs very selective shallow mechanical action near the crop or other soil-working benefits from the hoe, hoeing may still have a role and Zasso should be positioned as a complementary or alternative tool depending on the field fit."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso always replaces hoeing, always kills roots, or always reduces the number of passes. Hoeing can still be the better fit for very small weeds, certain crop stages, or highly selective shallow in-row work.

Do not imply that Zasso has zero operational constraints. Performance depends on weed species, growth stage, density, plant and soil moisture, residue, crop geometry, speed, contact quality, safety protocols, and local validation.

Do not make blanket economic claims. Whether Zasso is more efficient than hoeing depends on labor, number of repeat passes otherwise needed, regrowth pressure, field conditions, machine setup, traffic, and the value of avoiding soil disturbance.
