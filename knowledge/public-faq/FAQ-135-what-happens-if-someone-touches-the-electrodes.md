# What happens if someone touches the electrodes?

## Short Answer

No one should ever touch Zasso electrodes while the equipment is energized or in an active operating state. Direct contact with energized high-voltage electrodes, or with conductive material touching them, can create a serious electrical shock or electrocution hazard. If contact occurs or is suspected, the operator should stop high voltage immediately, follow the product-specific emergency and shutdown procedure, keep others away, and arrange medical evaluation or emergency assistance according to local safety rules.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding technology works by transferring controlled high-voltage energy through electrodes or applicators into target plants and the plant-soil pathway. The intended current path is through the vegetation and return path defined by the equipment and field conditions. A person touching an energized electrode could become part of an unintended electrical circuit, especially if they are also in contact with the ground, wet surfaces, metal, irrigation equipment, or another conductive object.

The seriousness of an electrical contact event depends on the current that flows through the body, the path that current takes, the duration of contact, contact conditions, available energy, waveform, footwear, moisture, and how quickly the machine shuts down. Because those variables cannot be safely judged by a bystander or salesperson in the field, the customer-facing rule should be simple: do not touch electrodes, conductive materials connected to electrodes, applicators, cables, or nearby conductive objects during operation unless the product manual and training explicitly define a safe shutdown and maintenance condition.

If someone touches an electrode during operation, it should be treated as a safety incident, not as a normal operating event. The operator should stop the high-voltage output immediately using the approved control or emergency stop procedure, prevent secondary exposure by keeping others away, avoid touching the person or equipment if there is any chance the system is still energized, and follow the product-specific emergency response instructions. After shutdown, the equipment should not be restarted until the situation has been assessed, any required medical or emergency response has been completed, and the machine and site are confirmed safe according to Zasso training and the applicable manual.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should build their operating procedure around prevention. People, animals, maintenance staff, and bystanders must stay outside the active treatment zone, and no one should approach or touch the electrodes while high voltage may be active. Operators should maintain line of sight or other approved site control, stop if someone enters the safety zone, and use barriers, signage, supervision, and training appropriate to the worksite.

For demonstrations, farms, municipalities, contractors, and distributors, this question is a useful way to explain why exclusion zones and training are not optional. The safest message is that electrode contact is a serious hazard and must be prevented by machine controls, worksite control, trained operation, inspection, shutdown discipline, and compliance with the product-specific manual.

The exact safe distance, PPE, shutdown sequence, discharge time, restart conditions, and maintenance procedure can vary by product configuration, country, certification, site, and operating condition. Customers should always rely on the approved Zasso manual and training for those details.

## Evidence and Context

Zasso's public technology material explains that high voltage is generated locally and transferred through applicators into plants and soil during application. This supports explaining electrode contact as contact with the active energy-transfer part of the system: https://zasso.com/technology/

Existing Zasso FAQ files in the GitHub repository, especially FAQ-132, FAQ-133, and FAQ-134, consistently frame high-voltage safety as a complete-system issue involving current path, available energy, exposure time, insulation, grounding or return-path management, fault detection, shutdown behavior, exclusion zones, training, maintenance, and field conditions.

Zasso internal safety materials reviewed for this FAQ identify direct contact with energized electrodes or conductive materials touching electrodes as a primary human-safety risk. They also support conservative mitigation language: keep people and animals outside the exclusion zone, stop high voltage when the zone is breached, prevent contact with conductive objects, avoid unsafe water or metal pathways, and rely on product-specific emergency, shutdown, maintenance, and restart procedures.

SharePoint safety-risk material reviewed for this answer describes direct contact by a person or animal with energized electrodes as a severe electrical hazard, and treats conductive materials, water paths, and metal objects touching electrodes as situations that can extend or redirect risk. Related internal safety writing emphasizes that the practical safety question is not raw voltage alone, but whether current paths are controlled, monitored, limited, and shut down quickly under abnormal conditions.

Michigan State University Extension describes electrical weed control as using a charged electrode and high-voltage electricity to treat weeds, with the treatment affected by voltage, current, speed, soil moisture, plant condition, and electrode contact: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

GROW IWM's weed-electrocution factsheet explains that weed-electrocution equipment transfers high-voltage current into plants and warns that improper handling can create severe shock risks for operators or nearby individuals. It points to training, protective equipment where required, safety distances, inspections, maintenance, and caution around wet or rainy conditions as important controls: https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

General electrical-safety guidance from OSHA and university safety programs supports the same risk logic. Shock severity depends on the amount of current, the path through the body, and the duration of exposure, with other factors such as contact conditions and frequency also relevant: https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/2022-01/Electrical%20Hazards%20in%20Construction%20Student%20Workbook.pdf and https://ehs.cornell.edu/book/export/html/1441

## Safe Sales Wording

"No one should touch the electrodes while the equipment is energized. Direct contact with energized electrodes or conductive materials touching them can be a serious electrical hazard."

"If contact occurs or is suspected, the operator should stop high voltage immediately, keep others away, and follow the product-specific emergency, shutdown, and medical-response procedure."

"This is why Zasso requires trained operation, exclusion zones, inspections, and strict adherence to the approved manual before operation, maintenance, or restart."

## Caveats

Do not say that electrode contact is safe, harmless, survivable, comparable to a small static shock, safe with ordinary footwear, safe for trained operators to test, or safe after shutdown unless the approved product procedure explicitly confirms the equipment is de-energized and safe to touch.

Do not give improvised medical, rescue, electrical, or maintenance instructions beyond the approved product manual and local emergency procedures. If exposure is suspected, use the product-specific emergency response process and seek appropriate medical or emergency assistance.

Do not disclose confidential voltage, current, frequency, pulse, discharge-time, sensor, shutdown-threshold, insulation-monitoring, certification, incident, or test-result details unless already approved for public use. Product-specific manuals and training take priority over this general FAQ.
