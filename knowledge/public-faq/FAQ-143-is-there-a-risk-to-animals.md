# Is there a risk to animals?

## Short Answer

Yes, animals can be at risk if they enter the active treatment area, touch electrodes or conductive machine parts, or become part of an unintended electrical path. Zasso equipment should be operated with animals kept outside the required safety zone, just as people and bystanders must be kept away during active high-voltage operation. The safest customer-facing answer is that animal risk is managed through exclusion, trained operation, site control, stopping high voltage when animals approach, and following the product-specific manual and local rules.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso electrical weeding uses controlled high-voltage energy to treat target plants through applicators or electrodes and the intended plant-soil pathway. Animals are not intended to be part of that pathway. The main risk is unintended electrical exposure: an animal could contact an energized electrode, conductive vegetation, a metal object, water path, fence, irrigation component, or machine part that has become energized during an abnormal condition.

The practical rule is simple: animals should not be allowed near the machine while high voltage may be active. This includes farm animals, working animals, wildlife, and domestic animals that may wander into the work area. The operator should maintain the required exclusion zone, control access before starting, stop the high-voltage output if animals enter the safety zone, and only restart when the area is clear and the approved procedure allows it.

Animal risk can be more difficult to manage than human bystander risk because animals may not respond to warnings, may move unpredictably, may be attracted by vegetation or water, and may contact the ground, plants, fences, or metal structures differently from people. Wet ground, puddles, irrigation, conductive fencing, metal objects, and damaged insulation can increase the importance of disciplined site control.

This does not mean Zasso equipment is unsuitable for farms, orchards, vineyards, roadsides, or other areas where animals may be present. It means the worksite must be planned so animals are kept away during operation, and the operator must be ready to stop if site control is lost. Product-specific manuals, training, safety distances, signage, barriers, supervision, and local requirements always take priority over general FAQ language.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should treat animals as protected bystanders. Before operating, they should check whether livestock, pets, working dogs, horses, wildlife, or other animals can access the treatment area. Gates, fences, handlers, spotters, warning signs, temporary barriers, or a simple pause in work may be needed depending on the site.

For farms and contractors, the key planning question is whether animal access can be controlled during treatment. In open fields this may be straightforward. In pastures, orchards, vineyards, public areas, municipal sites, roadside work, or properties near domestic animals, the operator should take extra care to prevent animals from approaching the applicators, treated vegetation, wet ground, metal objects, irrigation equipment, or the machine during active operation.

Customers should not assume that animal risk is solved only by distance or only by the animal not touching the visible electrode. The safe approach is to keep animals outside the approved safety zone and to stop the machine if there is uncertainty.

## Evidence and Context

Zasso's public technology material explains that high-voltage electricity is generated locally, transferred through applicators into plants and soil, and closes through another applicator or soil contact. This supports explaining animal safety around controlling the intended current path and preventing unintended contact: https://zasso.com/technology/

Existing Zasso FAQ files in the GitHub repository, especially FAQ-132 through FAQ-142, consistently frame safety as a layered system involving current path, available energy, exposure time, insulation, grounding or return-path management, fault detection, shutdown behavior, exclusion zones, training, maintenance, and field conditions. The bystander and maintenance-worker FAQs are especially relevant because animals should be kept out of the same active treatment area.

Internal Zasso safety and electrical-weeding guide material reviewed for this FAQ discusses high-voltage operation, application space, direct-contact risk, insulation monitoring, and the need to keep unintended persons or animals outside the active application zone. This internal material supports the high-level customer wording here, while product-specific distances, thresholds, engineering details, and procedures should remain in approved manuals and training.

Recent Read.AI meeting context reviewed for this FAQ reinforces that Zasso treats training, operation according to manuals, service support, and clear exclusions for misuse or unauthorized operation as important deployment topics. That supports explaining animal safety as a combination of equipment design, trained operation, site control, and manual-based procedures.

Michigan State University Extension describes electrical weed control as using charged electrodes and high-voltage electricity to treat weeds, with performance and behavior influenced by voltage, current, speed, soil moisture, plant condition, and electrode contact: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

GROW IWM's weed-electrocution guidance states that electrical weed-control equipment operates at high voltage and that improper handling can create severe shock risks for operators or nearby individuals. It recommends training, protective gear where required, safe distances, inspections, maintenance, caution around wet or rainy conditions, and maintaining a safe working perimeter: https://growiwm.org/weed-electrocution/ and https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

General farm electrical-safety guidance on stray voltage also supports the principle that animals can be affected when they contact two surfaces with a voltage difference or become part of a conductive path. While stray-voltage guidance is not specific to Zasso equipment, it reinforces the conservative recommendation to prevent animals from contacting energized or faulted conductive paths: https://www.alliantenergy.com/safety/farm-safety/stray-voltage

## Safe Sales Wording

"Animals should be kept outside the active treatment area whenever Zasso equipment is operating. If animals enter the safety zone, the operator should stop high-voltage output and follow the approved procedure before restarting."

"Zasso equipment is designed for trained professional operation, with safety distances and site control. Animals, like people, should never approach or touch the applicators, machine, conductive objects, or treated area while high voltage may be active."

"Exact safety distances and site-control requirements are product-specific, so customers should follow the Zasso manual, training, and local rules for their machine and application."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso technology is harmless to animals, safe for animals to approach, safe around livestock without exclusion, or safe for use in uncontrolled public or pasture settings. Do not imply that animals are protected simply because the equipment targets plants.

Do not provide product-specific safety distances, discharge times, PPE rules, emergency animal-handling instructions, sensor thresholds, insulation-monitoring details, rescue procedures, certification claims, or veterinary advice unless they are taken from approved customer-facing product documentation.

Animal risk can vary by product, applicator type, site layout, species and behavior, soil moisture, rain, irrigation, puddles, metal objects, fencing, terrain, vegetation, machine condition, local rules, and operator behavior. Customers should use approved manuals, training, site controls, and local support for the exact requirements.
