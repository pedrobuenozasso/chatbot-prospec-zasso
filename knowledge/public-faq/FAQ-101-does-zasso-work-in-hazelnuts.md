# Does Zasso work in hazelnuts?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can work in hazelnuts when the application is designed for the orchard layout, target weeds, crop-safety requirements, and local operating conditions. Hazelnut-specific research from Oregon has examined electrical weed control for Italian ryegrass in hazelnut orchards, including work connected to Zasso equipment, and supports the idea that electrical weeding can be a useful non-chemical tool in suitable conditions. The safest customer-facing claim is that hazelnuts are a promising, locally validated orchard use case, not that Zasso is proven to replace every herbicide, mowing, sucker-control, or pre-harvest floor-management pass.

## Detailed Answer

Hazelnuts are a logical fit for Zasso because commercial orchards need repeated vegetation control in tree rows, orchard floors, edges, sucker zones, young blocks, and pre-harvest areas. In many production systems, weeds can compete with young trees, interfere with irrigation, complicate orchard-floor preparation, and make sweeping, windrowing, pickup, drying, and nut-quality management more difficult when nuts are harvested from the ground.

Zasso's technology is contact-based. The electrodes must contact the target vegetation and avoid unwanted contact with crop tissue, trunks, roots, irrigation equipment, metal objects, workers, animals, and other sensitive infrastructure. In hazelnuts, the best fit is therefore targeted treatment of reachable weeds in defined zones such as tree rows, strips, problem patches, field edges, or pre-harvest cleanup areas, rather than indiscriminate whole-orchard treatment.

The strongest crop-specific evidence found for this FAQ is Oregon hazelnut work on electrical weed control against Italian ryegrass, a major weed and herbicide-resistance concern in the Pacific Northwest. Internal Zasso materials summarize Oregon State University field work in hazelnut orchards where electrical weed control using Zasso-linked equipment reduced Italian ryegrass reproductive output and was reported as comparable to herbicide application for several reproductive metrics under the tested conditions. Public reports also describe OSU research on electrical weed control in hazelnuts and emphasize integration into resilient weed-management programs.

For sales purposes, the practical answer is positive but conditional: Zasso can be considered for hazelnut orchards where weeds are emerged and reachable, where a trained operator can protect the crop and infrastructure, and where the customer values non-chemical control, herbicide reduction, resistance management, low-residue positioning, organic or reduced-herbicide programs, or pre-harvest floor preparation. It should be validated locally with side-by-side data before making crop-specific performance, crop-safety, or economic commitments.

## What This Means for Customers

Hazelnut growers should view Zasso as a potential additional tool in integrated orchard-floor management. It may be especially relevant where conventional herbicide, mowing, flailing, cultivation, flaming, organic contact products, or hand cleanup are insufficient, restricted, costly, or strategically unattractive.

A good hazelnut pilot should measure weed control, regrowth, crop safety, trunk and root protection, irrigation safety, sucker-zone effects, operating speed, fire-risk conditions, orchard-floor cleanliness, harvest readiness, and cost per treated hectare, acre, row meter, or service campaign. The comparison should be against the grower's actual current program, not only against the product cost of a single herbicide pass.

The strongest early use cases are likely mechanized orchards with clear row geometry and a defined pain point: resistant Italian ryegrass, difficult tree-row weeds, organic or low-residue production, pre-harvest cleanup, young-orchard competition, or problem-zone treatment.

## Evidence and Context

GitHub repository research found Zasso hazelnut crop and customer-profile materials that position hazelnut as a medium-priority orchard opportunity. Those materials identify clean orchard floors, harvest readiness, herbicide-resistance pressure, residue expectations, organic or reduced-herbicide needs, and local economics as the main reasons to test Zasso in hazelnuts.

Internal Zasso technical material summarizes Oregon State University work on nonchemical management of Italian ryegrass in hazelnut orchards. The summarized study involved Oregon field sites, electrical weed control using EH30 Thor / Zasso equipment, mowing, glufosinate, integrated treatment sequences, and untreated checks. The material reports that electrical weed control reduced Italian ryegrass reproductive metrics and was similar to herbicide application for several outcomes under the tested conditions, while noting that full numeric tables, crop-safety data, and economics were not available in the extracted text.

SharePoint search surfaced broad Zasso electrical-weeding guide and efficacy-library material, but no stronger customer-approved hazelnut commercial claim than the GitHub evidence. Recent Read.AI meeting context supported current Zasso focus on orchard/perennial-crop equipment, training, and service readiness, but did not provide a hazelnut-specific approved customer claim.

Public sources support the hazelnut context. Oregon State University Extension explains that orchard floors are prepared before nut drop and that weeds are flail-mowed to facilitate harvest in Oregon/Washington hazelnut systems: https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9223-integrated-pest-management-strategic-plan-hazelnuts-oregon-washington

OSU's orchard-floor guidance notes that tree-row herbicide programs and flailing between rows are common weed-management practices in Pacific Northwest hazelnuts: https://extension.oregonstate.edu/sites/extd8/files/documents/em9079.pdf

Public OSU/industry reporting describes electrical weed-control research in hazelnuts led by Marcelo Moretti and focused on herbicide-resistant Italian ryegrass, including the possibility of integrating electrical weed control into a more resilient hazelnut weed-management program: https://www.wcngg.com/2022/04/18/can-electricity-aid-in-the-fight-against-herbicide-resistant-italian-ryegrass-in-hazelnuts/

The Pacific Northwest Weed Management Handbook describes orchard, vineyard, and berry systems as commonly using mowing or flailing in row middles and herbicides in the crop row, while warning that repeated use of the same practices can shift weed populations: https://pnwhandbooks.org/weed/horticultural/orchards-vineyards/vegetation-management-orchard-vineyard-berry

## Safe Sales Wording

"Yes, Zasso can be suitable for hazelnut orchards where the target weeds are reachable and the equipment is configured to protect the trees, roots, suckers, irrigation, and orchard infrastructure."

"Hazelnut-specific research in Oregon supports electrical weed control as a promising non-chemical tool for difficult orchard weeds such as Italian ryegrass, especially as part of an integrated program."

"For hazelnuts, we recommend a local pilot that measures weed control, regrowth, crop safety, harvest-floor condition, operating speed, and economics before making broader performance commitments."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso is automatically safe for all hazelnut orchards. Electrical weeding is contact-based, so trunks, roots, suckers, irrigation lines, posts, wires, low branches, workers, and animals must be protected through equipment setup, operating procedures, and trained use.

Do not promise full herbicide replacement, permanent control, one-pass control, lower cost in every orchard, harvest-quality improvement, or universal organic approval. Results depend on weed species, weed stage, weed density, plant and soil moisture, soil conductivity, orchard geometry, floor condition, dry biomass, operating speed, electrode setup, safety procedures, and local regulations.

Dry vegetation, hot or windy weather, residue, mulch, and pre-harvest dry biomass can increase fire and operating risk. Local fire rules, Zasso operating guidance, emergency procedures, and site-specific no-go conditions should be followed.

The direct hazelnut evidence is promising but still incomplete for broad commercial claims. More crop-safety, economics, harvest-floor, regrowth, and multi-season data should be collected before presenting Zasso as a fully proven hazelnut system in every region.
