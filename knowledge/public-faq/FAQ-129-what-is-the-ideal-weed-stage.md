# What is the ideal weed stage?

## Short Answer

The ideal weed stage for Zasso is usually when target weeds are emerged, actively growing, physically accessible to the electrodes, and not yet so dense, woody, lodged, or reserve-rich that contact and energy delivery become difficult. In many situations, young to moderately developed weeds are attractive targets because they have less biomass and fewer recovery reserves than mature plants. The practical optimum is field-specific: it depends on weed species, weed height, crop stage, canopy or residue shielding, soil and plant moisture, applicator setup, speed, pass strategy, and the customer's agronomic goal.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding works through physical contact and controlled energy delivery. The best stage is therefore not defined only by the calendar or by a universal leaf number. It is the stage where the applicator can reliably contact the target vegetation and deliver enough energy to damage the shoot, growing tissues, root-shoot connection, and recovery structures before the weed becomes too difficult to treat.

For early post-emergence weed control, emerged young weeds can be favorable because they generally have thinner tissues, smaller root systems, and fewer stored reserves for regrowth. This timing can also help prevent weeds from becoming competitive, producing seed, or requiring a more intensive later treatment. However, very small seedlings can be missed if they sit below the electrode contact zone, are hidden by residue, are under a crop canopy, or are shielded by taller weeds.

For late-season weed escapes, a more developed stage can sometimes be useful when the weeds stand above the crop canopy and can be contacted without touching the crop. External research on weed electrocution has found strong performance in some later-stage applications because taller weeds made better electrode contact and because treatment could reduce seed viability. That does not mean mature weeds are always ideal. Mature, woody, branching, rhizomatous, tuberous, lodged, or very dense weeds can require slower operation, repeated passes, mowing before treatment, or integration with other tools.

The safest customer-facing rule is: treat when weeds are visible and contactable, before they become too dense or resilient, and while crop safety and electrode access can be maintained. For each field, the ideal stage should be validated with Zasso's product-specific guidance, local crop conditions, target species, and follow-up scouting.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should plan Zasso applications around contact quality and treatment objective. If the goal is early weed suppression, the best timing is usually after the target weeds have emerged and are accessible, but before they have built large biomass or regrowth reserves. If the goal is late-season escape control or seedbank reduction, the useful window may be later, provided there is enough height separation from the crop and the electrodes can contact the weeds safely.

A practical field check should ask: Are the target weeds emerged? Can the electrodes touch them consistently? Are they hidden by residue, crop canopy, mulch, or larger weeds? Are the weeds already woody, flowering, seeding, rhizomatous, or very dense? Are soil and plant conditions suitable for current flow? Will one pass be enough, or should the plan include slower speed, repeated passes, mowing before treatment, or follow-up scouting?

The efficacy chapter summarizes field examples including Italian ryegrass treated around 20-30 cm in a vegetative/boot stage, organic soybean weeds treated around 4-8 cm, and blueberry/perennial weed trials where speed, repeated passes, mowing before electrical treatment, and species response changed results.

Zasso's public website describes Electroherb as a non-selective electrophysical mode of action using a directed electrical circuit to target plants, and positions Zasso as a provider of electric weeding technologies for agriculture and municipalities: https://zasso.com/

Michigan State University Extension notes that electrical weeding is often used for late-season weed escapes and that weeds generally need to be taller than the crop when crop selectivity depends on height separation. It also lists weed density, plant moisture, soil moisture, weed morphology, contact time, electrode height, direction, speed, and number of passes as important performance variables: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

Michigan State University Extension also summarizes field-crop research where electrocution was most effective at later plant growth stages in a height-selective late-season context, most likely because more weeds contacted the electrode. The same source cautions that grasses showed less visual control and better recovery when treated earlier, and that plant height and plant moisture were highly correlated with performance in those studies: https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/electrical-weed-control-in-vegetables-and-field-crops

A Weed Science article evaluating electric weed control with Zasso XPower reports that plant species, plant age, soil type, weather, plant morphology, and physical electrode contact affect performance. It notes that seedlings are likely to be easier to control than mature plants, while mature plants can shield smaller plants and larger or branching plants may require sufficient current dose for full control: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/electric-weed-controlhow-does-it-compare-to-conventional-weed-control-methods/488C45B191B97E84E34257E80EB9CFC6

Oregon State University Extension reports that slower speeds improved electrical weed control in organic blueberry fields because electrodes had more time to transfer energy into plants. It also reports that mowing before electrical treatment improved energy contact and control in dense or tall weed growth, while sequential passes were useful for perennial regrowth: https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9716-how-speed-timing-affect-electrical-weed-control-organic-blueberry-fields

The Weed Science Society of America summary of soybean weed-electrocution studies reports that control was more effective in later growth stages in that study set and was closely related to plant height and plant moisture at treatment. It also notes reductions in seed viability once weeds had set seed, supporting late-season escape-control use cases without making that the universal ideal stage: https://wssa.net/2022/07/study-documents-the-effectiveness-of-electrocution-in-weed-control/

## Safe Sales Wording

"The best timing is when the weeds are emerged, accessible to the electrodes, and still manageable before they become too dense, woody, or reserve-rich."

"For early weed management, young emerged weeds are often a good target; for late escape control, taller weeds can also be suitable when there is safe height separation from the crop and reliable electrode contact."

"We define the ideal stage field by field, based on species, height, density, crop safety, soil and plant conditions, and the pass strategy recommended for the Zasso product being used."

## Caveats

Do not claim a universal ideal growth stage, universal weed height, or guaranteed one-pass control. Electrical weeding depends on physical contact and delivered energy, and the best stage varies by weed species, morphology, density, crop system, soil and plant moisture, applicator design, operating speed, and customer objective.

Do not imply pre-emergence or residual control unless discussing a specific validated pre-emergence application. Zasso generally treats contacted vegetation present during application and does not prevent later germination from the seedbank.

Avoid product-specific stage, speed, voltage, power, or dose recommendations unless they come from approved product guidance for the local crop and market. Do not disclose confidential machine settings, unpublished trial data, customer names, pricing, certification status, or private meeting details.
