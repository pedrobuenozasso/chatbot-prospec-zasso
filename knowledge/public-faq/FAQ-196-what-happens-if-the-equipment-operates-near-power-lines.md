# What happens if the equipment operates near power lines?

## Short Answer

Zasso equipment should not be operated near overhead or energized power lines unless the work has been planned and controlled according to the approved Zasso manual, local site rules, utility-owner requirements, and applicable regulations. Power lines are a severe electrocution hazard: dangerous contact can occur through direct contact, arcing, conductive machine parts, wet vegetation, irrigation equipment, tools, cables, or other objects that enter the required clearance zone. If the machine, tractor, applicator, cable, load, vegetation, or any other conductive object could approach a power line, the operator should stop, keep people away, and obtain qualified site-specific guidance before work begins or resumes.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso electrical weeding equipment is designed to apply controlled high-voltage energy through applicators or electrodes into target plants and the intended plant-soil pathway. Operating near external electrical infrastructure creates a different risk category. Overhead distribution or transmission lines may carry much higher energy than the Zasso treatment circuit, and the hazard is not limited to touching the visible wire.

The main risk is that part of the work system could enter the minimum approach distance for an energized line. That can include the tractor, folded or raised equipment, applicators, cables, tools, irrigation pipes, ladders, trailers, vegetation, metal crop infrastructure, spray or water paths, or bystanders. Depending on voltage, weather, moisture, equipment height, terrain, line sag, and local rules, electricity may arc through air or energize conductive parts and ground areas. If contact or arcing occurs, the machine and surrounding ground may become dangerous because of touch-voltage and step-voltage effects.

The safe customer-facing rule is conservative: identify overhead and nearby electrical lines before treatment, define the work zone, maintain the required clearance, and involve the utility owner or qualified electrical authority whenever work could approach the clearance zone. Operators should never estimate line voltage, measure line height casually, rely on visual judgment alone, or assume that insulated-looking overhead conductors are safe to approach. Many agricultural safety sources advise treating overhead lines as energized and maintaining clearance; formal distances vary by jurisdiction, voltage, equipment type, and task.

If the equipment contacts a power line, becomes entangled, or there is suspected arcing, the operator should treat it as an emergency. Keep everyone away from the machine, ground contact area, fences, irrigation parts, wet surfaces, cables, and nearby conductive objects. Do not approach, touch, rescue, move, fold, unfold, or service the equipment until the utility owner or qualified emergency personnel confirm the line and site are safe. The exact emergency response must follow the approved Zasso procedure, local emergency plan, and utility instructions.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should treat power lines as a pre-work planning issue, not as an obstacle to handle during treatment. Before operating, the site should be assessed for overhead lines, service drops, substations, transformers, utility poles, guy wires, irrigation pivots, trellis wires, field gates, public roads, rail corridors, and any work route where equipment could be raised, folded, transported, or turned near electrical infrastructure.

For farms, contractors, municipalities, rail or roadside teams, and distributors, the practical requirement is simple: keep Zasso equipment and all associated people, tools, cables, vegetation, and conductive infrastructure outside the required clearance zone. If the required clearance cannot be confidently maintained, the work should be redesigned, postponed, or coordinated with the utility owner. In some cases, the line may need to be de-energized, moved, guarded, or otherwise controlled by the utility or competent authority before work can proceed.

This also affects training and demonstrations. Operators and supervisors should know how tall the equipment is in all positions, including transport, turning, folding, unfolding, maintenance, loading, unloading, and field entry. They should also know who has authority to stop work when line clearance is uncertain.

## Caveats

Do not give a universal clearance distance as a substitute for the approved Zasso manual, local regulation, utility-owner instruction, or site-specific risk assessment. Minimum approach distances depend on country, voltage, line type, equipment type, task, terrain, weather, line sag, operator position, and whether the equipment is being operated, transported, folded, unfolded, lifted, maintained, or loaded.

Do not say that Zasso equipment is safe to operate under power lines, safe near all overhead lines, safe if the line appears insulated, safe if the machine does not touch the line, or safe because protective systems are present. Do not provide improvised rescue, power-line disentanglement, electrical repair, or restart instructions.

If a power-line contact, arcing event, entanglement, fire, shock, fallen conductor, energized fence, energized irrigation component, or suspected energized machine occurs, keep people away and follow the approved emergency procedure, utility instructions, and local emergency response process.

Do not disclose confidential voltage, current, power, pulse, frequency, clearance assumptions, grounding or bonding design, sensor thresholds, alarm logic, shutdown behavior, certification details, incident records, test results, customer sites, or product-specific service procedures unless already approved for public use.
