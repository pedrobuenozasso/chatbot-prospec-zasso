# What is a plant-soil-plant resistive circuit?

## Short Answer

A plant-soil-plant resistive circuit is a simple way to describe the electrical path created during some electrical-weeding treatments. Current can move from one electrode into a contacted plant, through its above-ground tissues and roots, across conductive soil pathways, and then through another contacted plant or return path back to the machine. The term matters because the plant, roots, soil, moisture, density, and electrode contact all affect where useful energy goes.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding works only when the target vegetation becomes part of a closed electrical circuit. In the field, that circuit is not a clean laboratory wire. It is a biological and soil-based pathway made from plant tissue, root architecture, soil moisture, soil conductivity, electrode geometry, and the return connection to the machine.

In a plant-soil-plant circuit, one contacted plant can act as part of the outgoing path and another contacted plant, root zone, soil path, or return device can help complete the circuit. Each part of that route has electrical resistance or impedance. Above-ground stems and leaves, crowns, roots, wet or dry soil, and neighboring plants can all either help carry current or divert it away from the intended target.

For customers, the most important point is practical: Zasso is not simply applying electricity to visible leaves. The goal is controlled energy delivery through the plant-soil system so enough useful energy reaches tissues that determine survival and regrowth. If the current path passes well through the target plant and root zone, the treatment is more likely to produce durable control. If the current is divided across many plants, lost through bare soil, reduced by poor contact, or affected by unsuitable moisture conditions, performance can be lower.

This is why field conditions matter. Weed size, species, density, root type, soil moisture, soil conductivity, contact time, application speed, residue, and electrode contact quality all influence the electrical load seen by the machine and the dose received by each plant.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should think of the treatment as a managed plant-soil circuit, not as a universal shock that behaves the same everywhere. Good results depend on matching the machine, applicator, operating speed, and treatment timing to the local crop or site, weed spectrum, soil condition, and safety requirements.

The concept also helps explain why training and scouting matter. Operators need to maintain good electrode contact, avoid unnecessary bare-soil bypass where product guidance requires it, and recognize when dense vegetation, wet conditions, heavy residue, or mature root systems may require adjusted expectations or follow-up management.

## Safe Sales Wording

"A plant-soil-plant resistive circuit means the weed, its roots, nearby soil, and the return path all form part of the electrical load. Zasso's application quality depends on guiding useful energy through the target plant, not just touching leaves with high voltage."

"This is why weed stage, soil moisture, density, electrode contact, and speed matter. The better the circuit through the intended plant tissue, the better the chance of durable control under suitable conditions."

"We evaluate electrical weeding as controlled energy delivery through a biological circuit, with trained operation and local validation to match the crop, weeds, and field conditions."

## Caveats

Do not claim that every treatment sends current equally through every root or guarantees complete root kill. Current follows available conductive paths, and useful energy can be reduced by poor contact, dense vegetation, unsuitable soil moisture, bare-soil bypass, residue, plant architecture, speed, or machine setup.

Do not present the plant-soil-plant model as a validated numeric formula for all crops, weeds, soils, or products. It is a helpful explanation of current paths and field variability, while exact settings, thresholds, machine behavior, and control logic are product-specific.

Do not disclose confidential machine settings, impedance thresholds, proprietary control algorithms, unpublished field data, customer-specific results, pricing, certification details, or sensitive electrode-design information.
