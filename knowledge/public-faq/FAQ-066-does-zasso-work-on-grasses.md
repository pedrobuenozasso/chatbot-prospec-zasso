# Does Zasso work on grasses?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can work on grass weeds when the target grasses can be contacted properly and the application is matched to their growth stage, density, moisture, and regrowth potential. Grasses can be more challenging than many broadleaf weeds because of their narrow leaves, protected growing points, tillering, dense stands, and root or rhizome reserves. The strongest claim is that grasses are treatable with Zasso in suitable conditions, not that every annual or perennial grass infestation will be controlled equally in one pass.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso's Electroherb technology uses controlled electrical energy through contacted vegetation. Because the mode of action is physical rather than herbicide-based, a grass weed is not protected simply because it is a grass, a monocotyledon, or herbicide-resistant. If the electrodes make good contact and enough useful energy reaches the plant tissues and recovery structures, grass weeds can be damaged and controlled.

Grass efficacy is still highly condition-dependent. Many grasses have narrow leaves, protected growing points, tillers, dense clumps, rhizomes, stolons, or fibrous root systems. These traits can make contact and durable control harder than for some upright broadleaf weeds. Dense grass mats can also shield smaller plants and divide the electrical load across many contacts. Mature or dry grasses, perennial grasses, lodged plants, or grasses with strong belowground reserves may need slower operation, better contact, repeated passes, mowing or other preparation, or integration with additional weed-management tactics.

Public and internal evidence supports careful confidence. Australian DPIRD work using Zasso XPower reported strong grass results in specific conditions, including high control of annual ryegrass and kikuyu when speed and conditions were suitable. The same body of evidence also cautions that broadleaf weeds were generally easier to control than grasses at higher speeds and that grass control varied with stage, density, moisture, and speed. Texas A&M organic cotton work summarized in Zasso SharePoint material also found broadleaf control to be very strong while grass control was more sensitive to speed and pass number.

For customers, the practical answer is: yes, include grasses in the target spectrum, but validate the protocol. The sales discussion should identify the grass species, whether it is annual or perennial, the growth stage, height, density, soil and plant moisture, crop proximity, operating speed, pass strategy, fire risk, and acceptable regrowth threshold.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers with annual grass weeds, resistant ryegrass, kikuyu, crabgrass, Johnsongrass, Brachiaria, or other grass problems should not rule out Zasso. The technology can be relevant where chemical options are restricted, herbicide resistance is present, soil disturbance should be limited, or residue-free vegetation management is valuable.

The best customer trial should separate grasses from broadleaf weeds in the assessment. Record grass species, stage, density, whether plants are tillering or mature, whether they are annual or perennial, soil and plant moisture, speed, number of passes, mowing history, and regrowth at 14 to 56 days. For perennial or rhizomatous grasses, success should be measured beyond immediate visible injury, because regrowth and reproductive output matter more than first-day burn-down.

## Evidence and Context

Zasso public materials describe Electroherb as a patented, non-chemical electric weed-control technology that applies electrical energy through the plant-soil pathway rather than through a chemical active ingredient: https://zasso.com/technology/

GitHub repository research reviewed FAQ-060 through FAQ-065 on efficacy, herbicide comparison, mechanical comparison, resistant weeds, and glyphosate-resistant weeds. Those answers consistently frame Zasso efficacy as dependent on target species, contact quality, delivered energy, speed, density, moisture, and follow-up management.

GitHub repository research also reviewed the Zasso plant-susceptibility note. That internal note states that Electroherb susceptibility is mainly driven by physical contact and energy delivery, and that both monocots and dicots can be susceptible when good applicator-to-leaf contact is achieved. The same note lacks quantified species-level efficacy data, so it supports mechanism framing rather than universal grass-control claims.

A GitHub field-trial note summarizing the 2024 DPIRD final report, "What is the best fit for electric weed control in Australia?", identifies Zasso XPower testing across annual ryegrass, kikuyu, couch grass, winter grass, annual veldt grass, silver grass, wild oats, and mixed vegetation. It reports that broadleaf weeds were generally easier to control than grasses at higher travel speeds, while grass performance improved when speed and conditions were appropriate. The note highlights condition-specific results such as strong control of wet dense kikuyu at slow speed and major biomass reduction of herbicide-resistant annual ryegrass.

SharePoint research reviewed the Electrical Weeding guide and efficacy-publication material. These sources summarize multiple efficacy studies and specifically note that grass weed control in an organic cotton context was more variable than broadleaf control and more sensitive to speed and pass strategy. They also summarize Italian ryegrass work in hazelnut orchards where electrical weeding reduced reproductive output and seed viability.

Recent Read.AI meeting summaries reviewed for operational context emphasize the importance of applicator design, contact quality, operating speed, operator training, certification, telemetry, and post-sales execution. These summaries support careful customer-facing wording: grass control depends on setup and disciplined operation, not simply on the technology category.

DPIRD's public project summary reports that broadleaf weeds were controlled more effectively than grass weeds, while mature grasses such as kikuyu and glyphosate-resistant annual ryegrass were controlled comparably to herbicide treatments under tested conditions: https://library.dpird.wa.gov.au/rqmsplant/935/

A Western Australia government article on the same DPIRD research states that the Zasso XPower machine was assessed for glyphosate-resistant annual ryegrass and that control of mature grasses such as kikuyu could be comparable with herbicide treatment, while also cautioning against dry-residue fire-risk conditions: https://www.wa.gov.au/government/announcements/australian-agronomy-conference-2024-dpird-research-sheds-light-electric-weed-control-potential-and-soil-health

A 2024 Australian agronomy proceedings paper states that broadleaf weeds were generally easier to control than grass species, and that dense mature weeds can shield other plants from contact; it also reports that grass control can be high when speed and conditions are appropriate: https://www.agronomyaustraliaproceedings.org/images/sampledata/2024/2024ASAborger_catherine_1-7-947-Borger-Catherine.pdf

Michigan State University Extension describes electrical weed control as an emerging method for late-season, escaped, and herbicide-resistant weeds and identifies application speed, contact time, plant moisture, soil moisture, density, and morphology as important performance factors: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

GROW IWM describes weed electrocution as a non-selective, non-chemical method that can be effective across a broad spectrum of weeds, while noting that weed density, species, plant moisture, soil moisture, and integration with other practices affect results: https://growiwm.org/weed-electrocution/

Oregon State University Extension field guidance in organic blueberry shows the same practical principle: slower speeds generally improved electrical weed-control performance, while repeated passes can help maintain control where weeds recover: https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9716-how-speed-timing-affect-electrical-weed-control-organic-blueberry-fields

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso can treat grass weeds, including difficult annual or resistant grasses, when contact quality, speed, growth stage, density, moisture, and follow-up strategy are properly managed."

"Grass control is often more parameter-sensitive than broadleaf control, so we recommend validating the protocol for the customer's grass species and field conditions."

"For dense, mature, perennial, or rhizomatous grasses, Zasso should be positioned as part of an integrated program, potentially including slower speeds, sequential passes, mowing, or other complementary practices."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso controls all grasses, controls grasses as easily as broadleaf weeds, eliminates rhizomes or stolons in every case, or guarantees one-pass control. Grass species, growth stage, density, moisture, soil conditions, crop proximity, contact quality, speed, and regrowth potential all matter.

Be especially careful with mature grasses, dense mats, lodged grasses, perennial grasses, rhizomatous or stoloniferous grasses, dry biomass, and high-fire-risk conditions. In those situations, the most credible recommendation may include slower operation, repeat passes, mowing before treatment, seasonal timing, or integration with chemical, mechanical, cultural, or scouting-based practices where allowed.

Do not disclose confidential machine settings, unpublished customer results, raw trial data, control logic, telemetry details, pricing, certification plans, or private meeting content unless explicitly approved for external use.
