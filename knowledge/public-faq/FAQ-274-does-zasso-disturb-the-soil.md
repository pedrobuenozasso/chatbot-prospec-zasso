# Does Zasso disturb the soil?

## Short Answer

No, not through its mode of action. Zasso's electrical weeding is designed to control contacted weeds without tilling, cultivating, inverting, uprooting, or mixing the soil, so it is best described as a minimal soil-disturbance weed-control method. That said, the tractor or carrier platform can still affect the soil through normal traffic, especially in wet or vulnerable conditions.

## Detailed Answer

Soil disturbance usually refers to physically moving, loosening, cutting, burying, or inverting soil. Conventional weed-control methods such as tillage, cultivation, hoeing, or aggressive under-row mechanical tools often work by disturbing the soil surface or root zone to uproot or bury weeds.

Zasso works differently. Its Electroherb technology applies controlled electrical energy through contacted vegetation and the plant-soil circuit. The weed-control effect does not depend on turning the soil, slicing through the root zone, or mixing soil layers. For that reason, the electrical treatment itself is not a soil-disturbing process in the way cultivation or tillage is.

This lower-disturbance profile can be valuable where customers want weed control without exposing bare soil, disrupting residue cover, or repeatedly working the soil around crop roots. That can be especially relevant in orchards, vineyards, citrus, berries, perennial crops, conservation-oriented systems, and fields where erosion or soil-structure protection matters.

The important distinction is that Zasso's electrical mode of action is minimal-disturbance, but the full field operation is not impact-free. Any machine pass can still influence soil through wheel load, axle load, tire pressure, repeated traffic, slope, and soil moisture conditions. The responsible customer-facing answer is therefore: Zasso does not disturb the soil as part of how it kills weeds, but the equipment still needs normal traffic and field-condition management.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, the main benefit is that Zasso can control weeds without the soil movement associated with many mechanical weed-control passes. That can support soil-cover preservation, reduced erosion exposure, no-till or reduced-till programs, and weed control in systems where repeated cultivation is undesirable.

In practice, customers should compare Zasso against the specific pass they are trying to replace. If the current pass mainly controls weeds by moving soil, Zasso may be a strong lower-disturbance alternative. If the current pass is also needed for seedbed preparation, residue incorporation, crust breaking, or other true soil-working functions, then the comparison is not one-to-one.

Customers should also manage the normal field-operation variables that still matter: avoid unnecessary passes, be careful on wet soils, match tires or tracks to field conditions, and validate the system under local crop, weed, residue, and terrain conditions.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso does not control weeds by tilling or moving the soil, so the electrical treatment itself is a minimal soil-disturbance process."

"Compared with soil-moving weed-control methods, Zasso can help customers manage weeds with much less direct soil disturbance, while still requiring normal traffic management for the machine pass."

"It is strongest where the customer wants weed control without cultivating, inverting, or repeatedly working the soil."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso has zero soil impact in every situation. The electrical mode of action is minimal-disturbance, but any field machine can still contribute to compaction or other traffic-related issues if soils are wet, loads are high, or passes are repeated.

Do not claim that Zasso always replaces every mechanical method. Some operations perform true soil-working functions such as seedbed preparation, residue incorporation, or crust management that Zasso is not designed to provide.

Do not overstate broader soil-health outcomes. The strongest supported claim is that Zasso avoids direct soil disturbance as part of the weed-control action, not that it automatically improves soil health or eliminates erosion or compaction risk in every field.
