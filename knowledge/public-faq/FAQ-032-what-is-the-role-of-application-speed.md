# What is the role of application speed?

## Short Answer

Application speed controls how long the electrodes stay in effective contact with each target plant. At a given power level and field condition, slower travel generally gives more time to transfer useful energy into the plant-soil circuit, while faster travel can improve productivity but may reduce dose per plant. The right speed is therefore not universal: it depends on the product, applicator, weed species, plant size, weed density, soil condition, safety requirements, and whether one slow pass or repeated moderate-speed passes is the better protocol.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding is a dose and contact process. The machine must make practical contact with the weed, close an electrical path through the plant and return circuit, and deliver enough useful energy to damage tissues that allow the plant to recover. Application speed affects that process because it changes exposure time.

If the machine moves too quickly for the target conditions, the electrodes may touch the weed for too short a period. That can produce visible injury without enough energy reaching crowns, roots, rhizomes, or other recovery structures. Faster travel can also make it harder to maintain consistent contact in dense, uneven, lodged, or mixed weed stands. In those cases, missed plants, shallow contact, shielding, or regrowth are more likely.

Slower travel usually increases contact time and delivered energy per plant or per treated area. That can improve control, especially when weeds are larger, perennial, grassy, dense, wet, or otherwise difficult to treat. However, slower speed also reduces field capacity and can increase operating cost per hectare or acre. In some situations, two or more moderate-speed passes can be more practical than one very slow pass, because repeat passes target regrowth and spread the operating burden across multiple treatment windows.

For Zasso, the safest sales message is that speed is an operating parameter to match to the field, not a single fixed number to promise in every crop. Operators should set speed according to validated product guidance, weed stage, target species, contact quality, soil moisture and conductivity, residue, crop proximity, fire risk, and follow-up strategy.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should think of speed as part of treatment quality. A machine that can drive faster is not automatically delivering better weed control if contact time becomes too short. The practical objective is to balance control, productivity, energy use, safety, and economics.

Before treatment, the field team should assess weed size, density, species, crop proximity, soil condition, terrain, residue, and fire risk. If the target is a dense or regrowth-prone infestation, a slower pass, better applicator positioning, pre-mowing in suitable situations, or sequential passes may be needed. If weeds are small, accessible, and conditions are favorable, a moderate operating speed may provide a better balance between efficacy and work rate.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Application speed matters because it determines how long the electrodes can transfer energy into each plant. We match speed to the weed stage, density, soil condition, applicator setup, and desired control level rather than relying on one universal speed."

"In many cases, a slower pass increases treatment dose, while repeated moderate-speed passes can be a practical strategy for regrowth-prone weeds. The right protocol depends on the crop, weed, equipment, and field conditions."

"Zasso focuses on controlled energy delivery and trained operation, so speed is selected as part of the full treatment plan, together with contact quality, power delivery, soil condition, and follow-up scouting."

## Caveats

Do not claim that slower is always better, that faster operation always fails, or that one speed applies across every product, weed species, crop, soil, region, and season. Speed interacts with power, contact time, electrode geometry, weed height, weed density, plant water content, root size, soil moisture, soil conductivity, residue, slope, crop proximity, fire risk, operator training, and pass strategy.

Do not promise one-pass control, guaranteed root kill, universal herbicide-equivalent performance, or a fixed hectare-per-hour productivity number without product-specific and field-specific validation. Avoid disclosing confidential speed settings, control logic, customer-specific trial results, pricing, certification details, telemetry data, or unpublished engineering parameters.
