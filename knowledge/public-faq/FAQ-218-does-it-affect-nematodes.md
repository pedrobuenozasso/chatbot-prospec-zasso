# Does it affect nematodes?

## Short Answer

Based on current Zasso materials and available external research, Zasso's electrical weeding is not expected to meaningfully harm free-living soil nematode communities when used under validated operating conditions. The strongest public support comes from DPIRD/Wine Australia work with Zasso XPower, which reported no evidence of damage to soil biota, including free-living nematodes, after tested applications. The claim should still remain qualified because nematodes are diverse, local conditions matter, and the evidence base is not yet a long-term global dataset across all soils, crops, species, and repeated-use programs.

## Detailed Answer

Nematodes are microscopic soil animals. Some are plant-parasitic pests, while many others are free-living organisms that feed on bacteria, fungi, or other small organisms and contribute to nutrient cycling and soil food-web activity. Because Zasso's technology sends electrical energy through the contacted plant and the plant-soil return path, it is reasonable for customers to ask whether nematodes in the surrounding soil could be affected.

The best current answer is cautiously reassuring. Zasso's Electroherb approach is a non-chemical, physical treatment that targets contacted weeds. It is not intended to fumigate, sterilize, heat, or chemically treat the soil, and it does not leave an active ingredient or residue behind. Exposure of nematodes depends on where they are in the soil profile at the moment of treatment, soil moisture, conductivity, root contact, weed density, application speed, energy dose, applicator design, and repeated-use history.

Zasso-held ecotoxicology material shows that nematodes were considered in early soil-organism testing and monitoring, alongside springtails, mites, earthworms, and microorganisms. That internal material frames nematode testing as potentially important and supports a field-monitoring approach rather than a broad laboratory chemical-toxicity model, because Electroherb has no molecular active ingredient and acts acutely through physical energy.

Public evidence is also encouraging. DPIRD's Australian project using Zasso XPower reported no evidence of damage to bacterial, archaeal, and fungal soil microbiome communities or free-living nematodes after electric weed control trials in 2022 and 2023. A related Agronomy Australia paper focused on Rhizoctonia solani AG8 as a soil-biota indicator and found no effect even at low application speed intended to maximize electrical dose and with repeated application at the same site. GROW Integrated Weed Management also summarized preliminary U.S. weed-electrocution research indicating no observed ill effects on soil microbial respiration, soil-dwelling arthropods, or soybean cyst nematodes in referenced trials, although that work includes non-Zasso equipment as well as Zasso-related evaluations.

This does not mean Zasso should claim that all nematodes are unaffected in every situation. Nematodes include many ecological groups and life strategies, and plant-parasitic nematodes associated with roots may not respond the same way as free-living nematodes in bulk soil. The responsible customer-facing position is that available tested evidence has not shown meaningful adverse effects on free-living nematodes under evaluated Zasso operating conditions, while local monitoring is appropriate where nematodes are a sensitive soil-health or crop-health KPI.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers focused on soil health, Zasso can be positioned as a residue-free, non-chemical weed-control option with encouraging soil-biota evidence. It may be especially relevant where the customer wants to reduce reliance on chemical weed control or soil-disturbing mechanical operations in suitable crops and field situations.

Where nematode health is important, the most practical approach is to evaluate free-living nematodes and plant-parasitic nematodes separately. Free-living nematodes can be used as a soil-food-web indicator, while plant-parasitic nematodes may be part of a crop-specific pest-management program. If a customer has a known nematode issue, Zasso should not be positioned as a nematicide unless specific local trials support that use.

For high-value crops, organic or regenerative programs, vineyards, orchards, sandy soils, or repeated-use programs, customers may choose to monitor treated and untreated areas using consistent sampling depth, season, soil moisture status, crop stage, weed density, application settings, and laboratory method.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Based on current Zasso materials and available public studies, tested Zasso applications have not shown meaningful adverse effects on free-living soil nematodes under evaluated conditions. We still recommend local validation where nematodes are an important soil-health or crop-health indicator."

"Zasso is a non-chemical, residue-free physical treatment. The responsible claim is compatibility with soil-biota goals under tested and validated conditions, not that every nematode species in every soil will always be unaffected."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso has zero effect on all nematodes in all conditions. Nematode response may depend on species or functional group, life stage, depth, proximity to roots, soil moisture, conductivity, organic matter, temperature, crop system, energy dose, application speed, electrode contact, weed density, and repeated-use history.

Do not position Zasso as a nematode-control product or nematicide unless supported by specific approved trials. Some plant-parasitic nematodes may be close to roots, but available evidence is not enough to claim reliable pest-nematode suppression.
