# Does it harm beneficial insects?

## Short Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding is not designed to target beneficial insects, and it does not apply insecticides, herbicide active ingredients, or chemical residues that would expose insects after treatment. Based on current Zasso materials and available public research, tested applications are generally reassuring for soil-dwelling arthropod indicators such as springtails and mites. The responsible answer is still conditional: beneficial insects are diverse, and risk depends on whether insects are directly on contacted vegetation, in the immediate treatment zone, or dependent on flowering weeds and habitat that are removed.

## Detailed Answer

"Beneficial insects" can mean many different organisms: predators such as lady beetles, lacewings, ground beetles, spiders and predatory bugs; parasitoid wasps and flies; pollinators; decomposers; and soil or litter arthropods such as springtails and mites. These organisms use different parts of the field, including soil pores, residues, roots, crop canopy, flowering weeds, hedgerows, field margins, orchard understories, vineyard alleys, and cover crops. Because the group is broad, Zasso should avoid a single absolute claim such as "no harm to beneficial insects."

Zasso's Electroherb technology is a physical, non-chemical weed-control method. Electrical energy is delivered through contacted vegetation and the plant-soil circuit at the time of application. It is not an insecticide, does not rely on a toxic active ingredient, and does not leave a chemical residue pathway for insects after the machine has passed. This is an important difference from many conventional pesticide exposure scenarios.

The strongest available evidence for beneficial arthropods is currently around soil and near-soil indicator organisms. Existing Zasso FAQ context and public research summaries report encouraging results for soil-dwelling arthropods such as springtails and mites under evaluated Zasso electric-weeding conditions. This supports a cautious statement that Zasso is compatible with soil-fauna goals when used under validated operating conditions.

That evidence should not be stretched too far. Beneficial insects include above-ground predators, parasitoids, pollinators, and insects living in flowers, stems, residues, or field margins. If an insect is physically present on a weed, flower, cover crop, or plant surface that is contacted by the electrodes during treatment, direct injury cannot be ruled out. If treatment removes flowering weeds or non-crop vegetation that beneficial insects use for nectar, pollen, shelter, alternative prey, or overwintering habitat, there can also be an indirect habitat or food-resource effect, even though no chemical residue is left behind.

For customer-facing use, the best position is: Zasso can reduce chemical exposure pathways and minimize soil disturbance compared with many alternatives, but beneficial-insect protection still depends on targeted application, timing, habitat management, local validation, and avoiding over-treatment of beneficial habitat.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, Zasso can be a useful tool in programs that want non-chemical weed control while reducing spray drift, chemical residues, and reliance on herbicide-only programs. It may be especially relevant in orchards, vineyards, organic systems, public areas, sensitive sites, and operations that want weed control with less soil disturbance than cultivation or tillage.

Customers should still manage the ecological role of vegetation. Some weeds are agronomic targets; others may temporarily support beneficial insects by providing pollen, nectar, alternative prey, shelter, or habitat connectivity. A good operating plan distinguishes the weeds that must be controlled from flowering strips, field margins, cover-crop zones, hedgerows, or biodiversity features that should be protected or treated at a different time.

In beneficial-insect-sensitive settings, customers should scout flowering status and insect activity before treatment, avoid treating flowers when beneficial insects are actively foraging, preserve designated habitat areas, and define local biodiversity goals clearly. Where beneficial insects are part of a formal IPM, regenerative, organic, biodiversity, or certification program, Zasso should be integrated into that program rather than presented as an automatic guarantee.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso is a non-chemical, residue-free physical weed-control technology, so it does not expose beneficial insects to pesticide residues after treatment. Available soil-fauna evidence is encouraging, but we still recommend habitat-aware timing and targeted application."

"The technology is designed to treat contacted vegetation, not insects. Beneficial-insect protection comes from avoiding active flowering habitat, preserving field margins where appropriate, and using site-specific IPM protocols."

"Zasso can help reduce chemical weed-control pressure, but we should not claim that any vegetation-control method is automatically harmless to every beneficial insect in every field situation."

## Caveats

Do not claim "no harm to beneficial insects" as an absolute statement. Direct injury cannot be ruled out if beneficial insects are on vegetation, flowers, residues, or soil surfaces that are physically contacted or within the immediate treatment path during application.

Do not treat springtail and mite evidence as proof for all beneficial insects. Soil microarthropods are useful indicators, but they do not represent every above-ground predator, parasitoid, beetle, lacewing, hoverfly, butterfly, bee, wasp, spider, larva, or ground-nesting insect.

Do not claim a biodiversity benefit solely because the method is non-chemical. Removing flowering weeds, cover crops, margins, or other non-crop vegetation can reduce nectar, pollen, shelter, alternative prey, and habitat connectivity if treatment is poorly timed or poorly targeted. Outcomes depend on crop, season, weed species, flowering stage, insect activity, soil moisture, residue cover, application speed, equipment setup, treatment frequency, and local conservation objectives.
