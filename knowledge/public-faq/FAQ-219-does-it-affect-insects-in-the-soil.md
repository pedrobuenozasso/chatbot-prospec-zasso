# Does it affect insects in the soil?

## Short Answer

Based on current Zasso materials and available external research, Zasso's electrical weeding is not expected to meaningfully harm soil-dwelling insects and related microarthropods when used under validated operating conditions. Zasso ecotoxicology work and public research summaries include springtails and mites, which are commonly used as soil microarthropod indicators, and the available evidence is generally reassuring. The claim should remain qualified because "soil insects" is a broad category, exposure depends on local conditions, and long-term data across all soils, crops, species, and repeated-use programs is still limited.

## Detailed Answer

Soil-dwelling insects and related arthropods include many different organisms, such as springtails, mites, beetle larvae, ants, and other small animals that live in soil pores, litter, roots, crop residue, and surface vegetation. Many of these organisms contribute to decomposition, nutrient cycling, soil food-web function, and pest regulation. Because Zasso's technology sends electrical energy through contacted plants and the plant-soil return path, customers are right to ask whether organisms living in or near the soil could be affected.

The best current answer is cautiously reassuring. Zasso's Electroherb technology is a non-chemical, physical weed-control method. It is not designed to treat the whole soil volume, fumigate the soil, heat the entire surface layer, or leave an active ingredient behind. Exposure of soil insects and microarthropods depends on whether they are in direct contact with the treated plant, roots, moist soil, litter layer, or electrode contact zone at the moment of application. It also depends on soil moisture, conductivity, plant density, application energy, speed, applicator design, organism size, organism mobility, and repeated-use history.

Zasso-held ecotoxicology material specifically considered soil fauna, including springtails and oribatid mites. In realistic field-use contexts, those materials report no or only minor effects at tested energy levels relevant to weed control. Higher acute effects were seen mainly in deliberately high-dose or model situations that were used to understand the mode of action rather than to represent normal operating practice. Selective or height-targeted applicator designs can further reduce unnecessary soil and litter exposure by treating only the plants that need to be controlled.

Public evidence points in the same direction but should not be overstated. A GROW Integrated Weed Management article summarizing preliminary U.S. research reported no observed ill effects on soil-dwelling arthropods such as springtails and mites in multi-state Zasso electric weeder evaluations. DPIRD work in Australia using Zasso XPower reported no evidence of damage to soil biota after electric weed control, although the specific public abstract focuses on microbial communities and free-living nematodes rather than insects. A broader Weed Science review describes electric weed control as a promising non-chemical technology while emphasizing that more short-term and long-term ecological research is still needed.

This means Zasso can responsibly say that tested applications have not shown meaningful adverse effects on common soil-fauna indicator groups under evaluated conditions. It should not claim that every insect, larva, nest, or soil arthropod in every possible field situation will always be unaffected. Ground-nesting insects, organisms directly touching treated vegetation, or organisms in very wet, conductive, high-contact microhabitats may have different exposure than organisms deeper in bulk soil.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers focused on soil health, Zasso can be positioned as a non-chemical, residue-free weed-control option with encouraging evidence for compatibility with soil-biota goals. It may be especially relevant where customers want to reduce herbicide reliance, avoid spray drift and residues, or reduce soil disturbance from mechanical weeding in suitable applications.

Compared with soil-moving tools, Zasso does not normally invert, cultivate, or mix the soil. That matters because many soil organisms depend on stable pores, litter, roots, organic matter layers, and surface habitats. In suitable crops and operating contexts, avoiding unnecessary soil movement can help preserve habitat structure even when weeds are being controlled.

Where soil insects or soil arthropods are a formal biodiversity metric, the responsible approach is local validation. Monitoring should compare treated and untreated areas using consistent sampling timing, season, soil moisture, crop system, sampling depth, organism group, application settings, and repeated-use history. For sensitive habitats or regulated biodiversity projects, the customer should define the target organism group clearly instead of using the broad phrase "soil insects."

## Evidence and Context

Zasso's internal ecotoxicology materials describe Electroherb as a physical, non-chemical process with no molecular active ingredient and no residue pathway. Those materials include soil-fauna screening and monitoring work involving springtails, oribatid mites, earthworms, nematodes, and microorganisms. The internal synthesis reports no or only minor effects under realistic field conditions, while noting that higher-dose model tests can produce stronger acute responses and that further monitoring is useful.

The same Zasso material notes that the method does not move soil and that treated plant material dries on site, which can help preserve habitat features for organisms that live in soil, tunnels, litter, nests, or surface residues when compared with more soil-disturbing methods.

GROW Integrated Weed Management summarized preliminary U.S. weed-electrocution research and reported that multi-state Zasso electric weeder evaluations were not showing ill effects on soil-dwelling arthropods such as springtails and mites, nor on microbial respiration rates: https://growiwm.org/after-the-volts-is-weed-electrocution-safe-for-the-soil/

DPIRD's project report, "What is the best fit for electric weed control in Australia?", evaluated electric weed control with a Zasso XPower unit and reported no evidence of damage to soil biota, including bacterial, archaeal, and fungal soil microbiome communities or free-living nematodes: https://library.dpird.wa.gov.au/rqmsplant/935/

A related 2024 Agronomy Australia/DPIRD paper using the Zasso XPower machine found that electric weed control did not influence the soil-biota indicator Rhizoctonia solani AG8, even at slow application speed and with repeated application at the same site. This is not an insect study, but it supports the broader soil-biota context: https://www.agronomyaustraliaproceedings.org/images/sampledata/2024/2024_ASAslaven_m_002-9-1107-Slaven-Miranda.pdf

A 2023 Weed Science review, "Exploring the potential of electric weed control," describes electric weed control as a developing non-chemical technology and emphasizes the need for more ecological research across soils, climates, organisms, application conditions, and time horizons: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/weed-science/article/exploring-the-potential-of-electric-weed-control-a-review/231EE50C385EF8962CDC46055C264237

## Safe Sales Wording

"Based on current Zasso materials and available public studies, tested Zasso applications have not shown meaningful adverse effects on common soil-fauna indicators such as springtails and mites under evaluated conditions. For sensitive biodiversity questions, we recommend local validation around the customer's crop, soil, and operating conditions."

"Zasso is a non-chemical, residue-free physical treatment. The responsible claim is compatibility with soil-biota goals under tested and validated conditions, not that every soil insect in every field will always be unaffected."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso has zero effect on all soil insects in all conditions. Soil insects and soil arthropods vary widely by species, size, life stage, mobility, depth, moisture exposure, nesting behavior, contact with roots or litter, and sensitivity to disturbance.

Avoid treating springtail and mite evidence as proof for every insect group. Ground-nesting insects, beetle larvae, ants, or organisms directly contacting treated vegetation may require separate evaluation if they are a customer-specific concern. Local soil moisture, conductivity, application speed, energy dose, electrode contact, weed density, crop residue, and repeated applications can all affect exposure.
