# What alarms does the machine have?

## Short Answer

Zasso machines can include visual, acoustic, display, and control-system alarms to warn the operator when the machine is not ready, when a safety condition is not met, or when an abnormal electrical or machine condition is detected. The exact alarms, icons, fault codes, reset rules, and service actions depend on the product, configuration, software version, market, and approved operating manual. For customers, the safest answer is that alarms are part of Zasso's layered safety concept, and any alarm should be treated as important until the operator has followed the product-specific procedure.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso equipment uses controlled high-voltage energy for vegetation control, so alarms are not just convenience messages. They are one of the ways the machine communicates readiness, abnormal conditions, safety stops, or required operator action. Depending on the machine and configuration, alarms may appear as visual warnings, acoustic warnings, indicator lights, display messages, fault codes, inhibit messages, shutdown messages, or service prompts.

At a customer-facing level, the relevant alarm categories may include safety alarms, high-voltage alarms, electrical fault alarms, machine-readiness alarms, sensor alarms, operational alarms, maintenance alarms, and emergency-related alarms. Examples can include emergency-stop activation, high-voltage enable or inhibit status, insulation or leakage warnings, grounding or earthing warnings, open-circuit or short-circuit conditions, over-current or abnormal power behavior, electrode position or ground-contact warnings, tilt or position warnings where fitted, speed or height detection warnings, communication or control faults, temperature or component-protection warnings, and maintenance or inspection reminders.

The exact alarm list should not be generalized across all Zasso products. A tractor-based applicator, vineyard machine, urban unit, prototype, manual applicator, regional configuration, or future product may have different sensors, display logic, alarm wording, fault codes, reset conditions, and service requirements. The approved manual and operator training for the delivered machine are therefore the authoritative source for the exact alarm names and actions.

The practical operating rule is simple: alarms should not be ignored or repeatedly reset without understanding the cause. If an alarm relates to safety, high voltage, insulation, grounding, damaged components, abnormal shutdown, emergency stop, water or metal contact, or unexpected machine behavior, operation should stop until the cause is understood and the approved procedure allows restart.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should expect alarms to support safer and more disciplined operation. They help the operator recognize when the machine is ready, when high-voltage treatment is blocked, when a safety condition has changed, when inspection or maintenance may be needed, and when trained support should be involved.

For farms, contractors, municipalities, distributors, and fleet owners, alarm handling should be part of operator training. Teams should know which alarms require immediate stop, which require inspection, who is allowed to reset the machine, when to call Zasso service or a trained technician, and how to document repeated or unresolved alarms.

For sales discussions, the important message is that Zasso equipment is designed to make important machine and safety states visible to the operator, but alarm details are product-specific. Customers should review the alarm table, troubleshooting section, and safety chapter for the exact machine they will operate.

## Caveats

Do not publish a universal Zasso alarm table unless it has been approved for the specific product and market. Do not claim that every machine has the same alarms, display messages, acoustic signals, fault codes, sensors, diagnostic coverage, logging behavior, reset rules, service escalation, response time, or certification basis.

Do not disclose confidential alarm codes, thresholds, electrical values, sensor layouts, control logic, software behavior, service procedures, validation results, supplier details, or customer-specific requirements unless already approved for external use.

An alarm does not automatically mean the machine is safe to touch, safe to restart, or safe for continued operation. Alarm interpretation depends on the machine state, product configuration, operator training, manual instructions, maintenance state, sensor condition, electrode and cable condition, grounding or return-path condition, soil and plant conductivity, water or metal exposure, terrain, bystanders, animals, and local rules.
