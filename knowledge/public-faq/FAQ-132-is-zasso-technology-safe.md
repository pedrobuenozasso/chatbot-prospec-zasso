# Is Zasso technology safe?

## Short Answer

Zasso technology is designed to be operated safely when used by trained operators, in the approved configuration, and according to the applicable product manual, safety distances, maintenance routines, and local rules. Because electrical weeding uses high voltage, it should never be described as risk-free or harmless. The safe customer-facing answer is that Zasso manages safety through engineered controls, controlled energy delivery, exclusion zones, training, inspections, and conservative operating procedures.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso's electrical weeding technology uses controlled high-voltage electricity to treat target plants through direct contact with applicators or electrodes. That makes it different from chemical weed control, but it also means the machine must be treated as professional high-voltage equipment. Safety depends on the whole operating system: the equipment design, insulation, grounding or return-path management, monitoring, fault handling, emergency shutdown, operator training, site preparation, maintenance, and field conditions.

In normal customer language, the right framing is not "safe in all circumstances." The right framing is "designed for safe professional operation when the correct procedures are followed." People and animals must stay outside the active treatment zone, operators must follow the manual and training, and the equipment should not be used through abnormal conditions such as damaged cables, damaged insulation, unexpected alarms, uncontrolled bystander access, unsafe contact with metal objects, active irrigation, puddles, or high fire-risk dry biomass unless the approved product guidance allows it and the risk is controlled.

Zasso's safety approach should be understood as layered protection. The physical design and controls aim to deliver energy into the intended plant-soil pathway, while operational procedures reduce the chance that a person, animal, metal object, water path, or fault condition becomes part of an unintended electrical path. Product-specific rules may vary by model, country, certification, applicator type, and use case, so customers should rely on the approved Zasso manual, training, and local support for the exact operating requirements.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should treat Zasso equipment like specialized agricultural or industrial machinery: safe operation is achievable, but it requires trained people and disciplined procedures. The operator should inspect the machine, maintain required safety distances, keep bystanders and animals away, stop for alarms or unusual behavior, avoid unsafe water or metal-contact situations, and follow lockout or shutdown procedures before maintenance.

The practical benefit is that customers can use a non-chemical weed-control method while managing the main electrical and operational risks through training, machine safeguards, site controls, and maintenance. This is especially important for farms, municipalities, contractors, and distributors who need repeatable safe operation across multiple operators and sites.

Customers should also expect safety rules to be product-specific. A compact machine, a tractor-mounted applicator, an urban-use configuration, and an agricultural field machine may have different safety distances, PPE, inspection routines, and operating restrictions. The approved manual and training always take priority over general FAQ language.

## Evidence and Context

Zasso's public technology material explains that high-voltage electricity is generated locally, transferred through applicators into plants and soil, and closes through another applicator or soil contact. It also describes the electrical effect as acting at the time of application and leaving no soil residue. This supports positioning Zasso as a controlled, non-chemical, high-voltage weed-control technology rather than as a chemical treatment: https://zasso.com/technology/

Existing Zasso FAQ materials in the GitHub repository consistently frame safety as a complete-system question. Prior answers on voltage peaks, frequency, open-circuit and short-circuit conditions, and electrical weeding explain that risk depends on voltage, current path, available energy, exposure time, grounding, insulation, fault detection, shutdown behavior, training, maintenance, and field conditions.

Zasso internal safety and electrical-weeding guide materials reviewed for this FAQ emphasize conservative operating controls: keep people and animals outside the active zone, prevent direct contact with electrodes and conductive materials, manage water and metal-object scenarios carefully, stop high voltage when exclusion-zone or water-path risks appear, and treat abnormal electrical conditions through approved procedures. These materials are useful for customer-safe framing, but product-specific thresholds and confidential engineering details should not be disclosed in an FAQ.

Recent Read.AI meeting summaries reviewed for current operating context reinforce that Zasso is prioritizing operator training, certification, post-sales support, product readiness, and service quality. That supports explaining safety as a combination of machine design and trained operation, not as a feature that exists independently of the operator.

Michigan State University Extension describes electrical weed control as using charged electrodes and high-voltage electricity to transfer current through weeds, roots, soil, and return or grounding equipment. It also highlights practical application variables such as voltage, current, speed, soil moisture, plant condition, and electrode contact: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

GROW IWM's weed-electrocution factsheet notes that electrical weed-control equipment operates at high voltage and that improper handling can create severe shock risks for operators or nearby individuals. It recommends training, protective gear where required, safe distances, inspections, maintenance, and caution around wet or rainy conditions and fire risk in dry vegetation: https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

Peer-reviewed and university sources describe electric weed control as a promising non-chemical tool but also identify safety and field-condition risks, including electrical hazards and fire risk under dry biomass conditions. This supports balanced customer language: the technology can be valuable, but safe use depends on the approved machine, operator procedures, and local conditions.

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso technology is completely safe, harmless, zero-risk, safe to touch, safe for untrained users, safe in all weather, safe around all metal objects or water, or safe without exclusion zones and procedures.

Do not disclose confidential voltage, current, frequency, pulse, module, sensor, interlock, shutdown, telemetry, diagnostic, certification, incident, customer, supplier, or test-threshold details unless they are already approved in public product documentation.

Electrical and operational risk can vary by product, applicator, crop or site, soil and moisture conditions, weather, vegetation dryness, nearby people or animals, metal objects, irrigation, maintenance state, local regulation, and operator behavior. Customers should follow the specific product manual, training, signage, PPE requirements, emergency procedures, and local compliance obligations.
