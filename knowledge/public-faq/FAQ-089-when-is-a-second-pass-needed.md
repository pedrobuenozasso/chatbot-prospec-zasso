# When is a second pass needed?

## Short Answer

A second pass is needed when one treatment is unlikely to deliver durable control or when follow-up scouting shows surviving weeds, regrowth, or new emergence that still matters for the customer's objective. It is most common with perennial weeds, grasses, dense or layered infestations, mature plants, difficult species, incomplete electrode contact, faster operating speeds, or crop-specific desiccation tasks where full dry-down is required. Zasso should not be positioned as always requiring two passes, but it should also not be sold as a universal one-pass solution.

## Detailed Answer

A second pass is a management decision, not a fixed rule for every field. The first pass should be planned to deliver enough useful energy through the target vegetation, with good electrode contact and enough contact time. If the target weeds are small, accessible, actively growing, and well contacted, one pass may be enough for the required level of suppression. If the first pass only damages top growth or leaves recovery tissues alive, a second pass can target surviving shoots, regrowth, or newly emerged weeds before they rebuild competition or seed production.

Second passes are especially relevant for weeds with strong recovery systems. Perennial weeds may regrow from crowns, rhizomes, tubers, runners, or deep roots. Grasses and prostrate species can be more difficult because growing points may be low, protected, or less exposed to the electrodes. Dense stands can shield smaller plants and split available energy across many plants. Mature, woody, or very tall vegetation may need an adjusted strategy because the electrical path and contact quality are harder to control.

Operating conditions also influence the decision. Faster travel speeds reduce contact time and can lower the energy delivered to each plant. Poor electrode contact, uneven ground, crop residue, mulch, canopy shielding, variable moisture, or high weed density can all make a single pass less reliable. In some programs, a moderate-speed first pass followed by a planned second pass can be more practical than one very slow pass, but this depends on the product, crop, weed spectrum, and operating window.

The best practical rule is to plan the first pass carefully, then scout. A second pass is justified when the field still contains economically or operationally meaningful survivors, regrowth-prone species, new flushes of weeds, or a desiccation target that has not reached the required result. Timing should be set by the agronomic objective and local protocol, not by a generic calendar claim.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should expect Zasso recommendations to be field-specific. A second pass may be part of the standard plan for difficult perennial weeds, mixed stands, dense infestations, grasses, prostrate weeds, or desiccation tasks where uniformity matters. In easier annual-weed situations, one well-executed pass may be sufficient.

The commercial value of a second pass is that it can protect the first treatment's result. It can reduce regrowth risk, catch missed plants, suppress newly emerged weeds, and improve uniformity where the customer needs a clean field, row, strip, or desiccation outcome.

Customers should build second-pass decisions into scouting and service planning. The question is not simply "one pass or two?" It is whether the treatment program has delivered the required result under the actual weed, crop, soil, moisture, speed, contact, and operating conditions.

## Evidence and Context

Existing Zasso FAQ materials on regrowth, regrowth reduction, speed, contact time, weed density, perennial weeds, grasses, mature plants, mowing, and expected control consistently frame pass number as condition-dependent. The main technical logic is that durable control depends on sufficient energy delivery to recovery tissues, not just visible foliar injury.

Zasso internal technical and marketing material reviewed for this FAQ supports the same principle: second-pass need is tied to root or recovery-tissue survival, plant morphology, operating speed, contact quality, weed density, soil and plant moisture, and the difference between immediate visual symptoms and durable suppression. Recent Read.AI context reviewed for this run did not add a specific pass-number claim, but it reinforced the importance of trained operation, field testing, product configuration, and customer education.

Zasso public technology material describes Electroherb as a non-chemical electrical mode of action that targets unwanted plants through controlled energy delivery: https://zasso.com/technology/

Oregon State University Extension reports that slower electrical weed-control passes provided stronger control in organic blueberries, while two moderate-speed passes could perform as well as, or better than, one slow pass. It also states that sequential passes are useful for perennial weeds that tend to regrow: https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/em-9716-how-speed-timing-affect-electrical-weed-control-organic-blueberry-fields

Frontiers in Agronomy reported that sequential passes in organic highbush blueberry improved control by 6-8 percentage points over single passes at equivalent energy, helping sustain control over time by targeting regrowth and new emergence: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/agronomy/articles/10.3389/fagro.2025.1688961/full

GROW IWM summarizes perennial-weed electrocution work indicating that repeated passes, or mowing combined with electrocution, can reduce perennial weeds and may be needed when regrowth pressure is high: https://growiwm.org/slow-the-cycle-targeting-perennial-weeds-with-weed-electrocution/

Texas A&M AgriLife organic cotton reporting found strong broadleaf control across many inter-row electrical-weeding treatments, but grass control was more sensitive to speed and pass number, with faster one-pass treatments performing worse than lower-speed or two-pass approaches: https://agrilifeorganic.org/2025/07/23/insights-on-inter-row-electrical-weeding-as-a-non-chemical-weed-management-tool-in-organic-cotton/

Michigan State University Extension explains that electrical weed-control performance depends on contact time, tractor speed, electrode height, plant moisture, soil moisture, slope, weed species, weed morphology, and number of passes: https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/basics-of-electrical-weed-control

## Safe Sales Wording

"A second pass is recommended when the weed, field, or operating conditions make one pass less likely to deliver durable control, or when scouting shows meaningful survivors or regrowth."

"For perennial weeds, dense patches, grasses, prostrate species, or desiccation work, we often plan for follow-up rather than assuming every field is a one-pass job."

"The right pass strategy depends on the target weed, growth stage, contact quality, speed, moisture, crop context, and the customer's required result."

## Caveats

Do not promise that one pass is always enough, and do not claim that two passes are always required. Pass number depends on weed species, plant size, growth stage, recovery structures, density, electrode contact, application speed, soil and plant moisture, crop constraints, applicator setup, local protocol, and the customer's required control threshold.

Avoid giving universal second-pass timing, speed, energy, or treatment-interval recommendations unless Zasso has approved guidance for the specific product, crop, weed, region, and field condition. Do not disclose confidential machine settings, control logic, unpublished trial data, customer-specific outcomes, pricing, certification details, or private roadmap information.
