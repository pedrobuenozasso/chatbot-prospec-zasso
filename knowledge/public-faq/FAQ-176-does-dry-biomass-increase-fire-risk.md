# Does dry biomass increase fire risk?

## Short Answer

Yes. Dry biomass is one of the clearest factors that can increase fire risk during electrical weeding. Dry weeds, straw, mulch, crop residue, mowed material, or desiccated cover crops can ignite more easily if sparks, arcing, hot spots, or unstable electrode contact occur. Zasso equipment should therefore be used only under approved operating conditions, with trained operators, field inspection, local fire rules, and fire-response readiness in place.

## Detailed Answer

Dry biomass matters because it can act as fuel. Electrical weeding is designed to move controlled electrical energy through plants and the intended return path, but field conditions are never perfectly uniform. If the electrode contact is unstable, if a small air gap forms, if residue is loose and dry, or if conductive debris changes the current path, short sparks or localized hot spots may occur. When those ignition sources are close to dry plant material, the chance of smoke, flame, or fire spread is higher than in green, moist, or low-residue conditions.

The risk is not simply whether a field contains weeds. The higher-risk situation is dry, continuous, or loose combustible material near the active treatment zone: dry standing weeds, dead grass, straw, dry cover-crop residue, mowed biomass lying like a mat, dry mulch, dust, or dead leaves. Fine dry fuels can ignite and spread more easily than moist vegetation, especially when weather is hot, windy, drought-affected, or low in relative humidity.

For customers, the practical rule is conservative: dry biomass should trigger a pre-work fire-risk assessment. Operators should check biomass moisture, residue load, fuel continuity, wind, temperature, humidity, drought or fire-danger restrictions, machine condition, electrode condition, abnormal sparking, and availability of suitable fire-response resources. If the site is outside approved limits, the correct decision may be to delay treatment, change timing, remove or manage residue first, or use another weed-control method.

Zasso should not be presented as fire-risk-free in dry biomass. It should be presented as a professional, protocol-driven non-chemical technology that requires the right site conditions, trained operators, maintained equipment, and disciplined stop-work procedures.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should pay special attention after mowing, after cover-crop termination, during drought, in summer heat, in straw or mulch, and in fields with dry residue loads. These are the situations where dry biomass can turn a small spark or hot spot into a safety issue.

A good operating plan should include a walk-through or visual inspection before treatment, confirmation that local fire restrictions allow the work, clear operator responsibilities, exclusion zones, monitoring during operation, and immediate stop-work rules for smoke, flame, repeated sparks, sustained arcing, abnormal odor, unusual noise, alarms, or damaged components.

This does not mean every dry-looking field is automatically impossible to treat. It means dry biomass is a material risk factor that must be assessed against the specific product manual, local regulation, weather, fuel load, and Zasso-approved operating guidance.

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso eliminates fire risk in dry biomass. Do not claim that electrical weeding is safe in all dry, hot, windy, drought-affected, straw-covered, mulch-covered, or residue-heavy conditions. Do not provide customer-specific thresholds for biomass moisture, humidity, wind speed, temperature, fire-danger rating, suppression equipment, or restart procedure unless they come from approved product manuals, Zasso safety guidance, local regulation, or customer site rules.

Fire risk depends on product configuration, electrode design and condition, power control, application speed, contact stability, biomass moisture, residue load, fuel continuity, soil condition, weather, wind, local fire rules, operator behavior, maintenance condition, nearby conductive objects, and emergency readiness.

If there is smoke, flame, repeated sparking, sustained arcing, abnormal odor, damaged insulation, damaged cables, damaged electrodes, contact with conductive debris, or uncertainty about field safety, operation should stop and the approved shutdown, inspection, and return-to-service procedure should be followed.
