# Is there residual charge after shutdown?

## Short Answer

There can be residual electrical energy after shutdown, depending on the product design, operating state, and field condition. For that reason, Zasso equipment should not be assumed safe immediately after the high-voltage output stops. Operators and service personnel must follow the approved product-specific shutdown, discharge, isolation, and verification procedure before touching electrodes, cables, applicators, conductive parts, or nearby conductive material.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding equipment uses high-voltage energy to move current through the intended treatment path: applicators or electrodes, target vegetation, soil or return path, and the power system. When the operator stops active application or switches the machine off, the treatment output is intended to stop, but shutdown alone is not the same as verified absence of hazardous energy.

A conservative safety approach is required because electrical systems can include stored energy, charged components, batteries, cable capacitance, fault states, wet or conductive residue, energized vegetation or metal objects, or restart risk. The practical answer for customers is simple: residual charge and other hazardous-energy conditions must be treated as possible until the product-specific procedure confirms the machine is safe.

This does not mean that every Zasso product remains energized for a long time after use. It means that safe access is procedure-based, not guess-based. The approved Zasso manual and training for the specific machine define what the operator must do, who is allowed to do it, what PPE is required, and how the safe state is verified.

For normal users, visitors, bystanders, and untrained workers, the rule is: do not touch the electrodes or high-voltage area after shutdown unless a trained and authorized person has confirmed that the equipment is safe according to the manual. For maintenance or service work, hazardous energy must be controlled before inspection, cleaning, clearing, repair, replacement, transport preparation, or other work near the high-voltage system.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should train operators not to rely on informal signs such as stopped movement, no visible sparks, no sound, dry-looking electrodes, or a briefly elapsed waiting time. The correct field habit is to stop the application, secure the area, follow the approved shutdown and discharge process, prevent restart where required, and verify the safe condition before access.

This is especially important after alarms, emergency stops, unusual arcing, contact with metal, wet conditions, damaged insulation, blocked electrodes, cleaning, maintenance, or transport preparation. In any uncertain situation, people should remain outside the safety zone and contact trained Zasso support or qualified service personnel.

The customer benefit is a clear and repeatable safety routine: treat residual energy as possible until the machine-specific process proves otherwise. That protects operators and bystanders while keeping sales and support language accurate and defensible.

## Caveats

Do not publish exact discharge times, stored-energy values, voltage or current thresholds, capacitance values, interlock behavior, service steps, lockout details, PPE ratings, or verification methods unless they are already approved for customer-facing use.

Do not tell customers that electrodes are safe immediately after shutdown. Do not imply that pressing stop, switching off high voltage, waiting briefly, hearing no sound, seeing no spark, or touching only briefly proves safety.

Residual-energy and safe-access requirements can vary by product, applicator, power architecture, battery system, cable condition, moisture, conductive residue, nearby metal, fault state, maintenance task, local regulation, and operator training level. The approved Zasso manual and training for the specific product always take priority.
