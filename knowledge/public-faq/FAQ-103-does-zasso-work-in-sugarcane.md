# Does Zasso work in sugarcane?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can be relevant for sugarcane, especially for directed inter-row weed control, early-season weed pressure, ratoon or escape cleanup, field edges, and organic or low-residue programs. The strongest fit is where the equipment can contact the weeds while avoiding the cane crop, stools, roots, irrigation, residue hazards, and other field infrastructure. Zasso should not be presented as a proven universal sugarcane herbicide replacement; sugarcane use should be validated locally with measured weed control, crop safety, operating speed, residue conditions, and economics.

## Detailed Answer

Sugarcane has several characteristics that make electrical weeding a plausible fit. It is a wide-row crop, early canopy closure is relatively slow, and weed competition during establishment or ratoon regrowth can be commercially important. In many cane systems, growers already use integrated programs that combine residual herbicides, directed sprays, cultivation, manual cleanup, residue management, and field-edge control. Zasso can add a physical, non-chemical mode of action where chemical control is constrained, resistant weeds are costly, labor cleanup is expensive, or sustainability and residue goals matter.

The central technical point is crop avoidance. Zasso's Electroherb technology controls contacted vegetation through electrical energy; it does not chemically distinguish sugarcane from weeds. In sugarcane, the practical use case is therefore directed treatment of weeds between rows, along edges, in patches, or in other validated treatment zones where the cane crop is protected. Crop stage, row spacing, cane height, stool and root position, residue load, soil moisture, weed species, weed density, and application speed all matter.

Internal Zasso materials support sugarcane as a serious test opportunity, including market analysis, target-customer positioning, and a historical cane-applicator concept for early inter-row weed control. Those sources support the commercial logic for sugarcane, but they should not be treated as completed sugarcane field-performance proof. A separate Zasso-linked technical report on electrical discharge in soybean and sugarcane nematode work showed biological activity but also severe crop-safety limitations under those test conditions, which reinforces the need to protect cane plants and validate any crop-use protocol carefully.

The best customer-facing position is positive but bounded: Zasso can be a promising sugarcane tool in suitable, locally validated applications, especially as part of integrated weed management. It should be sold first through pilots or structured demonstrations rather than as a blanket replacement for established cane herbicide programs.

## What This Means for Customers

For sugarcane growers, mills, contractors, and research partners, Zasso is most relevant where the weed-control job is valuable enough to justify a non-chemical physical pass. Good examples include resistant escapes, early inter-row weeds, ratoon cleanup, organic or low-residue cane, field margins, irrigation banks, mill-adjacent vegetation, and sustainability-backed chemical-reduction programs.

A good sugarcane evaluation should compare Zasso against the customer's real current program: herbicides, directed sprays, cultivation, manual cleanup, mowing, untreated control, or combinations of these. The pilot should measure weed suppression and regrowth, cane crop safety, stool or ratoon impact, operating speed, residue and fire-risk behavior, soil and plant moisture, energy or fuel use, electrode wear, labor need, and cost per treated hectare or treated strip.

The economic case is most credible when Zasso is compared with high-value problems such as labor-intensive cleanup, resistant weeds, rescue operations, chemical-reduction targets, or organic production. In many conventional cane systems, herbicide programs can be inexpensive and effective, so Zasso should be positioned as a targeted complement or premium tool before stronger substitution claims are made.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso can be relevant in sugarcane where the treatment is directed at weeds and the cane crop is protected, especially for early inter-row weeds, resistant escapes, ratoon cleanup, field edges, or low-residue programs."

"For sugarcane, we recommend a measured pilot before making performance or economic commitments, because crop safety, residue, weed stage, soil moisture, operating speed, and row geometry all affect results."

"Zasso should be viewed as a non-chemical tool within integrated sugarcane weed management, not as an automatic replacement for every herbicide or cultivation pass."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso is crop-selective in sugarcane. Electrical weeding is contact-based, so sugarcane plants, stools, young shoots, roots, irrigation equipment, metal objects, residue, and infrastructure must be protected according to the validated product setup and operating protocol.

Do not claim universal sugarcane efficacy, one-pass control, full herbicide replacement, guaranteed crop safety, proven sugarcane ROI, or current commercial availability of a dedicated sugarcane configuration unless those claims are supported by approved Zasso evidence for the specific country, machine, crop stage, and protocol.

Sugarcane results can depend strongly on plant cane versus ratoon cane, row spacing, cane height, weed species, weed height, weed density, soil moisture, plant moisture, soil conductivity, trash blanket or residue load, slope, field access, operating speed, safety procedures, and operator training.

Heavy dry biomass, green-harvest residue, drought, wind, and dry field edges can increase operational and fire-risk concerns. Local Zasso safety guidance, customer safety procedures, and no-go conditions should be followed.
