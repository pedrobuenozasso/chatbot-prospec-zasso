# Does speed affect efficacy?

## Short Answer

Yes. Application speed affects efficacy because it changes how long the electrodes stay in effective contact with each target plant and how much useful energy can be delivered into the plant-soil circuit. If the machine travels too quickly for the weed, crop, soil, applicator, and power setting, the treatment may cause visible injury but leave enough living tissue for regrowth. Slower travel can improve dose and contact time, but the best speed is always product- and field-specific.

## Detailed Answer

Electrical weeding depends on contact, current flow, and delivered energy. The target plant must become part of a practical electrical circuit, and the electrodes need enough time to transfer useful energy into the tissues that determine survival, including stems, crowns, roots, rhizomes, runners, or other growing points. Speed matters because it directly affects this exposure time.

At a given power level, applicator setup, and field condition, faster travel generally reduces the time available to deliver energy to each plant. That can be useful for productivity when weeds are small, accessible, and conditions are favorable, but it can reduce efficacy when weeds are mature, dense, lodged, grassy, perennial, wet, shielded by residue, or difficult to contact. In those situations, the result may look strong shortly after application while still allowing regrowth later.

Slower travel usually increases contact time and the dose delivered per plant or per treated area. This can improve control, especially where the objective is deeper injury rather than simple top-growth damage. However, slower is not automatically better in every case. Very slow operation reduces field capacity, may increase cost per hectare, can interact with crop proximity or fire-risk management, and may not be the most practical strategy when sequential moderate-speed passes are planned.

For Zasso, speed should be treated as an operating parameter to match to the field. The right setting depends on the equipment, applicator geometry, power delivery, electrode contact, weed species, weed stage, root volume, weed density, soil moisture, soil conductivity, residue, slope, crop clearance, safety rules, and the customer's control target. Operators should follow validated product guidance and use field scouting after treatment to confirm whether speed, contact quality, and pass strategy are producing the intended result.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should not judge a treatment plan only by hectares per hour. A faster pass can be attractive operationally, but it is not valuable if the target weeds do not receive enough useful energy for durable control. Speed should be selected together with power, contact quality, weed stage, soil condition, safety limits, and follow-up strategy.

In practical terms, difficult fields may need slower travel, better applicator positioning, residue management, mowing before treatment in suitable situations, or sequential passes. Easier fields with small, upright, accessible weeds may allow more productive speeds while still delivering acceptable control. The strongest commercial protocol is the one that balances efficacy, productivity, safety, energy use, and economics under the customer's actual conditions.

Speed also helps explain why results can vary between fields. The same machine may perform differently when weeds are taller, wetter, denser, more mature, more difficult to contact, or growing in a soil condition that changes current flow. A good field review should therefore ask whether the selected speed allowed enough contact time for the biological target.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Yes, speed affects efficacy because it changes contact time and delivered energy. We select speed according to the weed stage, density, soil condition, applicator setup, safety requirements, and desired control level."

"A slower pass can improve treatment dose in difficult conditions, while repeated moderate-speed passes can sometimes be the more practical strategy. The right speed is validated by product guidance and field conditions, not by a universal number."

"Zasso focuses on controlled energy delivery and trained operation, so speed is managed together with electrode contact, power delivery, soil conditions, and follow-up scouting."

## Caveats

Do not claim that slower speed always guarantees control, that faster speed always fails, or that one speed applies across all crops, weeds, soils, products, seasons, and operating objectives. Speed interacts with power, contact time, electrode geometry, weed height, weed density, plant water content, root size, soil moisture, soil conductivity, residue, slope, crop proximity, safety constraints, fire risk, operator training, and pass strategy.

Do not publish confidential machine settings, control logic, unpublished trial data, customer-specific results, pricing, certification details, telemetry, or internal engineering parameters. If a customer needs a numerical operating speed, provide only product-approved guidance or arrange a technical review for the specific application.
