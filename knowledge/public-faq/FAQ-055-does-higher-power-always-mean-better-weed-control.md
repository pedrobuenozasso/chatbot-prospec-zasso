# Does higher power always mean better weed control?

## Short Answer

No. Higher power can be useful only when the system delivers useful energy through the target plant and its recovery structures under suitable operating conditions. Weed-control results depend on delivered dose, contact time, electrode contact, plant size, weed density, soil moisture, soil conductivity, operating speed, and safety controls, not on the headline power rating alone.

## Detailed Answer

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is delivered. In electrical weeding, that matters because plant injury depends on whether enough useful energy reaches the tissues that allow the weed to recover, such as crowns, roots, rhizomes, or vascular pathways. A system with more available power may have more capacity to treat demanding conditions, dense vegetation, larger plants, or wider applicators, but that capacity only helps if the energy is directed through the right biological pathway.

Higher power does not automatically mean better control. Energy can be divided across several weeds, diverted into wet or conductive soil, lost through poor electrode contact, applied for too short a time at high travel speed, or spent damaging above-ground tissue without enough effect on recovery structures. In those cases, a larger power number may increase energy use, machine stress, or safety and fire-management requirements without delivering proportionally better agronomic results.

The better way to explain Zasso technology is controlled power delivery. Voltage, current, power, pulse behavior, electrode design, contact time, machine speed, plant morphology, and soil conditions all work together. The goal is not simply to apply the maximum possible power; it is to deliver enough controlled energy into the plant-soil circuit, for long enough, while staying within the product's validated operating and safety envelope.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should avoid comparing electrical weed-control systems only by kilowatts or generator size. More power can support larger working widths, tougher vegetation, or higher throughput in some configurations, but it does not replace correct setup, trained operation, suitable field conditions, and follow-up assessment.

A more useful buying question is whether the complete system can deliver repeatable treatment dose for the customer's target weeds, crop or site, soil condition, working speed, safety requirements, and service model. For example, dense weeds can split available power across many conductive paths, wet soil can change where energy flows, and faster travel reduces exposure time. Those practical variables often matter more than the power rating by itself.

For customers, the commercial message is that Zasso focuses on controlled, field-guided energy delivery rather than brute force. Product selection, applicator width, power configuration, operator training, maintenance, and application guidance should be matched to the real use case.

## Caveats

Do not claim that higher power always improves efficacy, guarantees root kill, eliminates regrowth, reduces the number of passes, improves economics, or makes one Zasso configuration universally better than another. Power must be interpreted with delivered energy, voltage, current, contact time, impedance, electrode geometry, plant species, plant size, plant water content, weed density, soil moisture, soil conductivity, residue, speed, safety systems, product setup, and operator training.

Do not disclose confidential Zasso power-module settings, power limits, control algorithms, fault thresholds, telemetry behavior, measured waveforms, unpublished field data, certification details, customer-specific results, supplier details, or pricing. Public language should stay at the concept level unless approved product manuals, technical sheets, or public materials support a more specific claim.
