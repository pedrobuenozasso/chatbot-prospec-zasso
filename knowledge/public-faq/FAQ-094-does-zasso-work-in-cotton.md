# Does Zasso work in cotton?

## Short Answer

Yes, Zasso can be relevant in cotton, especially for inter-row weed control, non-chemical weed-management programs, and selected applications where cotton plants can be protected from electrode contact. Electrical weeding is non-selective, so it should not be presented as an over-the-top cotton treatment unless the specific equipment, crop stage, row spacing, and operating protocol have been validated locally. Cotton is also a crop where post-harvest stalk destruction may be a promising adjacent use case, but that should be discussed separately from in-season weed control.

## Detailed Answer

Cotton is a row crop, which makes it a plausible fit for electrical weeding in defined treatment zones. The most credible in-season use is inter-row weed control: the electrodes treat weeds growing between cotton rows while the crop is protected by row geometry, guidance, crop stage, applicator design, and operator protocol. This can be valuable in organic cotton, herbicide-reduction programs, fields with resistant weeds, or systems where growers want a physical mode of action with less soil disturbance than repeated cultivation.

The central limitation is crop selectivity. Zasso's technology controls contacted vegetation through electrical energy; it does not distinguish a cotton plant from a weed by species. If cotton plants are contacted by the electrodes, crop injury is possible. For that reason, cotton applications must be designed around weed contact and crop avoidance, and they should be validated under the customer's row spacing, weed spectrum, crop stage, soil and plant moisture, residue conditions, and safety requirements.

Public cotton-specific research is encouraging but still application-specific. Texas A&M AgriLife Organic reported work with a Zasso-linked inter-row electrical weeding prototype in organic cotton, supported by AgriLife Research and Cotton Incorporated. The reported early conclusion was that inter-row electrical weeding showed promise as a non-chemical row-crop tool, performed as well as inter-row cultivation on broadleaf weeds, minimized soil disturbance, and required slower travel speeds when grass weeds were present.

Cotton stalk destruction is a separate but important discussion. In some cotton regions, destroying stalks after harvest is part of pest-management and compliance programs, especially where boll weevil host-free periods are required. Electrical treatment may have potential for cotton stalk destruction or crop-residue termination because the technology acts on contacted plant tissue, but Zasso should not claim regulatory compliance, complete stalk kill, or pest-eradication performance unless a locally approved protocol and evidence support that claim.

## What This Means for Customers

For cotton growers, Zasso should be positioned as a potential tool within integrated weed management rather than a universal replacement for herbicides or cultivation. The strongest customer discussion is where weeds are emerged between rows, the crop row can be avoided, and the grower wants a non-chemical tactic that can reduce soil movement compared with mechanical cultivation.

A practical cotton evaluation should start with the field geometry and target problem. Key questions include row spacing, crop growth stage, weed height, weed species, weed density, grass versus broadleaf pressure, soil and plant moisture, residue load, guidance accuracy, safety procedures, operating speed, and whether in-row weeds will need a complementary tactic. If the goal is stalk destruction, the discussion should shift to post-harvest timing, stalk size, regrowth risk, pest-management requirements, and local compliance rules.

The commercial message should be careful and useful: Zasso can help cotton growers add a physical, non-chemical mode of action in suitable cotton systems, but performance and crop safety depend on the validated application protocol.

Existing Zasso FAQ material consistently frames row-crop use as conditional: electrical weeding can work when the target vegetation is contactable, crop contact is avoided, speed and contact time are suitable, and local field conditions support energy delivery.

GitHub field-trial material reviewed during this run included the DPIRD/CNH/Zasso Australian field-validation note on XPower use. That source is not a cotton-specific efficacy trial, but it shows how Zasso field evidence should be interpreted: results depend on use case, target species, speed, moisture, residue, fire risk, and economics. It also identifies Inside Cotton / CRDC as one public distribution route for the Australian electric weed-control report, showing cotton-sector interest in non-chemical weed-control options.

SharePoint search was run for cotton, electrical weeding, cotton stalk destruction, row crops, and XPower, but no directly relevant cotton-specific document was returned in this run. Recent Read.AI meeting summaries reviewed in this run did not provide a cotton-specific approved claim. Those internal sources therefore did not add a stronger Zasso-approved cotton claim.

Zasso's public technology page describes Electroherb as a direct, non-chemical, residue-free physical mode of action for unwanted plant control: https://zasso.com/technology/

Texas A&M AgriLife Organic reported that a Zasso-linked inter-row electrical weeding prototype is being tested in cotton, with support from AgriLife Research and Cotton Incorporated. The article states that the approach showed promise as a non-chemical weed-control option in row-crop systems, performed as well as inter-row cultivation on broadleaf weeds, minimized soil disturbance, and showed reduced grass control at faster travel speeds: https://agrilifeorganic.org/2025/07/23/insights-on-inter-row-electrical-weeding-as-a-non-chemical-weed-management-tool-in-organic-cotton/

GROW IWM's weed-electrocution factsheet describes electrical weed control as a non-selective, non-chemical method and notes that performance is influenced by weed density, species, and plant and soil moisture. It also frames electrocution as a supplemental tactic rather than a standalone solution in most systems: https://growiwm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Weed-Electrocution-Factsheet-PDF.pdf

GROW IWM also reported row-crop proof-of-concept research using Zasso-linked systems in corn and soybean. While not cotton-specific, it supports the same row-crop logic: electrocution substantially reduced weeds between rows, but weeds hidden in the crop row remained a limitation, and crop damage was linked to direct contact: https://growiwm.org/researchers-test-novel-weed-electrocution-devices-in-row-crops/

Texas Department of Agriculture describes cotton stalk destruction as a pest-management program intended to eliminate cotton plants capable of hosting boll weevil populations and maintain a non-hostable period. This supports why post-harvest cotton stalk destruction is agronomically and regulatory relevant, but it does not by itself validate Zasso for that purpose: https://texasagriculture.gov/Regulatory-Programs/Cotton-Stalk-Destruction

UC IPM describes cotton weed management as an integrated program using cultural, mechanical, biological, and chemical methods, and notes the importance of controlling vegetation around field margins and similar areas that can contribute weed seed: https://ipm.ucanr.edu/agriculture/cotton/integrated-weed-management/

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso can be relevant in cotton where the application is designed to contact weeds while avoiding cotton plants, especially in inter-row or other validated treatment zones."

"For cotton, the strongest fit is as part of integrated weed management: a non-chemical physical mode of action that can complement cultivation, herbicides where allowed, and other local practices."

"Cotton stalk destruction is a promising discussion area, but any claim about regulatory compliance or complete destruction should be tied to local validation and approved protocols."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso is selective to cotton plants. Crop safety depends on avoiding direct electrode contact with the crop and using the right machine setup, row spacing, guidance, timing, and operating procedures.

Do not claim universal cotton efficacy, one-pass control, complete grass control at all speeds, full in-row control, season-long weed control, guaranteed yield protection, or automatic replacement of herbicides and cultivation. Grasses may require slower application, better contact, or follow-up tactics, and weeds hidden in the crop row may not be treated by an inter-row pass.

Do not claim cotton stalk destruction compliance, boll-weevil program compliance, complete regrowth prevention, or pest-management certification unless the local protocol, equipment, evidence, and regulatory approval support that specific claim.

Do not disclose confidential machine settings, unpublished trial data, customer names, private meeting details, pricing, product-roadmap details, or non-public commercial timelines.
