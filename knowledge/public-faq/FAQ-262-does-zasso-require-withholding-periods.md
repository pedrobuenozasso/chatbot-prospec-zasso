# Does Zasso require withholding periods?

## Short Answer

Generally, not in the pesticide-label sense. Zasso's electrical treatment does not apply a chemical herbicide active ingredient, so the treatment itself does not create the usual residue-based withholding period associated with a pesticide application. That said, customers still need to respect normal operating safety, crop-specific suitability, buyer rules, and any withholding periods that may still apply to other chemical products used elsewhere in the program.

## Detailed Answer

"Withholding period" can mean slightly different things depending on the country and crop, but in agricultural practice it normally refers to a residue-based waiting period after using a chemical product. In some markets the equivalent term is pre-harvest interval, harvest interval, grazing interval, or export interval. The common idea is the same: a minimum time must pass between the last chemical application and harvest, grazing, feeding, or sale so residues remain within allowed limits.

That matters because Zasso does not work by applying a herbicide molecule. The weed-control effect is electrical and physical rather than chemical. Electroherb applies controlled electrical energy through contacted vegetation instead of spraying a pesticide active ingredient onto the crop, soil, or harvested commodity. Because no herbicide chemistry is applied in the Zasso treatment itself, the treatment does not normally create a pesticide-style withholding period from that pass in the usual residue sense.

That is the commercially useful answer, but it needs careful boundaries. "No pesticide-style withholding period from the Zasso pass itself" is not the same as "no restrictions of any kind." Operators still need to follow product-specific electrical and machine-safety procedures during operation and shutdown. Suitability also still depends on the crop, the treatment zone, crop contact risk, harvest timing, and the customer's broader compliance environment.

The answer is especially conditional in mixed programs. If the grower also applies herbicides, desiccants, insecticides, fungicides, fertilizers with use restrictions, veterinary products on grazing systems, or other regulated inputs, those separate products can still impose their own withholding periods, pre-harvest intervals, export intervals, grazing restrictions, or buyer-imposed waiting periods. Local law, packer protocols, certifier expectations, food-safety plans, and customer contracts may also impose their own harvest or access rules even where Zasso itself does not.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, the practical value is that replacing a chemical weed-control pass with Zasso can remove the residue-based waiting period that would otherwise come from that avoided chemical application. That can be useful in residue-sensitive systems, export-oriented crops, public-facing operations, organic-compatible programs, orchards, vineyards, berries, and other settings where timing of harvest, scouting, mowing, irrigation, or labor access matters.

The safe field message is narrower than "harvest anytime with no checks." A better commercial explanation is: Zasso generally does not create a pesticide-style withholding period because it is a non-chemical treatment, but customers should still confirm the full use situation, including crop sensitivity, machine safety, any other inputs applied, and any local market or certification requirements.

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso removes every harvest, grazing, export, or access restriction in every market. The defensible claim is narrower: the Zasso treatment itself generally does not create a pesticide-style withholding period.

Do not use this FAQ as universal legal or regulatory advice. Terminology and compliance frameworks vary by country, crop, buyer, certifier, and use case.

Do not ignore other products in the program. If herbicides or other regulated inputs are used before or after the Zasso pass, their own withholding periods or pre-harvest intervals may still apply.

Do not confuse residue-related timing rules with machine-safety requirements. During operation and shutdown, the equipment still requires trained use, controlled access, and product-specific safety procedures.
