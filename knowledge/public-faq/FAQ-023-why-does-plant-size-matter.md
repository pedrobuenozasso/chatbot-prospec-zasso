# Why does plant size matter?

## Short Answer

Plant size matters because electrical weeding is a contact-and-energy process. Larger plants usually have more above-ground biomass, larger stems or crowns, and often larger root systems, so they may need more contact time, more delivered energy, or repeat treatment to achieve durable control. Smaller plants can be easier to treat when they are well contacted, but very small, hidden, low-growing, or shielded plants can also be missed if the electrodes do not touch them properly.

## Detailed Answer

Zasso's technology works by making the target vegetation part of an electrical circuit. The applicator must contact the plant well enough for useful electrical energy to move through leaves, stems, crowns, roots, and the surrounding return path. Plant size changes that process in several ways.

A larger plant generally has more tissue to damage and a larger biological recovery system. Thick stems, mature crowns, rhizomes, tubers, stolons, or large root systems can require more energy than a small seedling with limited reserves. In practical terms, the machine may need slower speed, better electrode contact, more suitable applicator geometry, or a second pass to deliver enough useful energy into the plant and recovery tissues.

Plant size also affects contact quality. Taller or broader plants may be easier for the applicator to touch, and sliding electrodes can sometimes remain in contact with them longer at the same driving speed. However, large, dense, branched, lodged, or tangled vegetation can also shield lower stems, growing points, or neighboring plants. That is why plant size should be considered together with weed density, species morphology, growth stage, soil moisture, electrode setup, power delivery, and operator speed.

The key message is not simply "small is good" or "large is bad." The right message is that the required treatment intensity changes with plant size and architecture. Good field assessment helps match the operating setup to the actual vegetation in front of the machine.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should expect better results when target weeds are at a size and growth stage that allow good electrode contact and adequate energy delivery. Very mature weeds, dense patches, grasses with protected growing points, and perennial weeds with large underground reserves may need slower operation, repeat passes, mowing before treatment, or integration with other weed-management tools.

For operations teams, plant size is a planning variable. Before treatment, it is useful to assess average weed height, canopy density, stem thickness, growth stage, root or regrowth risk, and whether the applicator can contact the plant consistently. After treatment, follow-up scouting is important because visible top damage does not always prove that the plant's recovery tissues received enough energy.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Plant size matters because electrical weeding depends on contact time and delivered energy. Larger or more mature weeds may need slower travel, better contact, or repeat treatment, while smaller weeds can be easier to treat when the applicator reaches them properly."

"Zasso equipment is designed to manage the plant-soil electrical circuit, but field results still depend on weed size, density, species, soil conditions, speed, and trained operation."

"For best results, we assess the vegetation first and match the operating setup to the weed stage and field conditions."

## Caveats

Do not claim that large weeds are always controlled in one pass, that small weeds are always easy, or that plant height alone determines efficacy. Plant size interacts with species, morphology, root reserves, moisture, density, electrode contact, soil conditions, speed, power delivery, and applicator setup.

Avoid giving universal speed, height, dose, or pass-number recommendations without product-specific and crop-specific guidance. Do not disclose confidential machine settings, unpublished test data, customer-specific results, certification details, pricing, or internal control logic.
