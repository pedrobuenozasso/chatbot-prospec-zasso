# Is Zasso more expensive than herbicides?

## Short Answer

Sometimes yes. In many conventional use cases, especially where herbicides are still effective, inexpensive, and easy to apply, Zasso can be more expensive than a herbicide program on a near-term cost basis. The right message is not that Zasso is always more expensive, but that it can carry a higher cost in some applications while still making strategic or total-program sense in others.

## Detailed Answer

A fair comparison is not only the price of herbicide product or the cost of one spray pass. It is the total cost of achieving acceptable weed control in the specific crop, site, and operating system. That includes machine ownership or service cost, annual utilization, labor, tractor time, energy or fuel use, application speed, maintenance, number of passes, safety procedures, regrowth management, and whether the herbicide program is simple or already becoming more complex.

In many standard spray situations, herbicides may remain cheaper. This is especially true when the chemistry still works well, the field operation is straightforward, the farm covers large hectares quickly, and the customer is comparing against a relatively low-cost conventional herbicide pass. Electric weed control can require a higher capital commitment, slower operating speed, trained operators, and sometimes multiple passes, all of which can make Zasso more expensive in that scenario.

The public research base supports that tradeoff. A 2025 Weed Science field study in vineyards found weed-control performance comparable to herbicides, but the electric treatment was applied at substantially slower speeds than the herbicide treatment. A broader 2024 Weed Science review also notes that electric weed control has historically been seen as less cost-effective, more energy-intensive, and less efficient than conventional chemical methods in some settings, especially where scale and speed dominate the economics.

That does not mean Zasso is a bad economic choice. The comparison can shift when the herbicide alternative is no longer truly simple or cheap, such as when resistance increases treatment intensity, when repeated under-row management is needed in permanent crops, when residue or drift constraints matter, or when the realistic alternative is manual labor or a disruptive mechanical program. In those cases, Zasso may still be competitive or favorable on a total weed-control-cost basis, even if it looks more expensive than herbicides in a narrow direct-cost comparison.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should assume that Zasso can be more expensive than herbicides in some situations and should ask for a use-case-specific business case instead of a slogan. That is the most credible and commercially useful answer.

In practice, Zasso is most likely to look more expensive when herbicide performance is still good, acreage is large, application windows are tight, machine utilization is low, or the comparison is made only against a low-cost spray pass. It is more likely to look competitive when the customer values reduced chemical reliance, needs a non-chemical mode of action, faces resistance or drift pressure, or would otherwise need labor-intensive or mechanically disruptive alternatives.

The safest sales approach is to compare one real customer program at a time: which herbicide pass is being replaced or reduced, how many hectares will be treated each year, how many electrical passes are expected, what speed is realistic, and what the customer is actually trying to optimize: direct cost, operational flexibility, resistance management, residue profile, or access to sensitive areas.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Yes, Zasso can be more expensive than herbicides in some conventional programs, especially when the spray alternative is still simple, fast, and low cost."

"The better message is that economics depend on the application. In the right use cases Zasso can be competitive or better, but we should not promise that against every herbicide program."

"We should compare the total cost of weed control for the real customer situation, including passes, labor, speed, utilization, and the actual alternative being replaced."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso is always more expensive than herbicides. That would be as misleading as claiming it is always cheaper.

Do not compare Zasso only to herbicide product price. Total cost depends on machine utilization, labor, power or fuel use, speed, number of passes, service model, maintenance, and the complexity of the alternative herbicide program.

Do not make crop-specific or customer-specific cost claims without a local business case. The economics can change materially by geography, weed pressure, crop system, annual treated area, and whether the real alternative is a simple spray program, a resistance-management program, or a non-chemical control program.
