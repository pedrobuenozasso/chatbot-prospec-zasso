# Is the efficacy comparable to mechanical weeding?

## Short Answer

Zasso's efficacy can be comparable to mechanical weeding in selected, well-matched applications, but the comparison depends on the weed, crop, growth stage, contact quality, soil and moisture conditions, operating speed, pass number, and the mechanical method used. In some public trials, electrical weeding performed similarly to cultivation for broadleaf inter-row control and outperformed mowing for biomass reduction in vineyard conditions. The safest customer-facing message is that Zasso can replace or complement certain mechanical passes where non-chemical control is needed, especially when customers want less soil disturbance, but it should not be presented as universally superior to mowing, tillage, hoeing, or cultivation.

## Detailed Answer

Mechanical weeding is not one method. It includes mowing, cultivation, hoeing, tillage, hand removal, under-vine tools, inter-row tools, and other implements that cut, uproot, bury, or disturb weeds. These methods can be effective, especially on small or young weeds and where the tool can reach the target without harming the crop. They can also have limits: some methods mainly remove above-ground biomass, some disturb soil or roots, some struggle in high-residue or stony conditions, and some are difficult around crop crowns, trunks, drip lines, or shallow roots.

Zasso's Electroherb technology works differently. It uses controlled electrical energy delivered through plant contact to injure the target plant through a physical, non-chemical mode of action. That can make it competitive with mechanical weeding where the goal is to control emerged weeds while reducing soil movement, crop-root disturbance, erosion risk, or repeated mowing. It is especially relevant in orchards, vineyards, organic systems, residue-sensitive settings, and integrated weed-management programs where a physical, non-chemical tool is valuable.

The evidence supports a conditional answer. In a public vineyard study using Zasso XPower equipment, electric weed control at 24 or 36 kW reduced weed biomass by 84% to 87% in the tested spring conditions, compared with 65% for mowing. In Texas A&M organic cotton work, inter-row electrical weeding gave broadleaf control above 95% and was generally comparable to cultivation, while grass control was more sensitive to speed and pass number. Oregon State University blueberry work shows another practical pattern: mowing before electrical treatment can improve electrical contact and efficacy in tall or dense weeds, meaning Zasso and mechanical methods may be complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

## What This Means for Customers

Customers should compare Zasso against the mechanical job they actually need to replace or improve. A fair comparison should define the target weed species, weed size, density, crop geometry, treatment timing, soil condition, speed, number of passes, crop-safety requirements, regrowth, cost, labor, fuel or energy use, erosion risk, and whether soil disturbance is acceptable.

Where mechanical tools already deliver cheap, fast, crop-safe control, Zasso may be a complement rather than the default replacement. Where mechanical tools disturb soil, miss regrowth structures, create erosion or crop-root risk, require scarce labor, or cannot easily work around sensitive crop structures, Zasso may offer a stronger value proposition.

## Safe Sales Wording

"In selected applications, Zasso can deliver weed control comparable to mechanical weeding while reducing soil disturbance, but the right comparison depends on the crop, weed spectrum, machine setup, speed, pass number, and field conditions."

"Zasso is often strongest where customers need a non-chemical physical tool but want to avoid repeated mowing, cultivation, hand labor, or soil movement."

"For tall or dense weeds, a mechanical step such as mowing before electrical treatment may improve contact and make the combined program stronger than either method alone."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso is always better than mowing, tillage, hoeing, cultivation, hand weeding, or other mechanical methods. Do not promise one-pass control, complete root kill, no regrowth, crop safety in every configuration, or universal cost advantage.

Mechanical comparisons are highly context-specific. A mower, inter-row cultivator, hoe, under-vine implement, targeted tillage tool, or hand crew may perform very differently depending on weed stage, soil condition, crop geometry, slope, residue, stones, weather, operator skill, and local cost structure. Zasso comparisons should be made against the customer's actual mechanical benchmark and measured with side-by-side field protocols.

Do not disclose confidential field data, customer names, raw trial files, machine settings, control logic, telemetry, pricing, certification timelines, or private meeting content unless specifically approved for external use.
