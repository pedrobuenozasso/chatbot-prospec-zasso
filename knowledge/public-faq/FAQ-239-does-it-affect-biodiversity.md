# Does it affect biodiversity?

## Short Answer

Zasso can support biodiversity goals in suitable applications because it controls vegetation without applying herbicide active ingredients, leaving chemical residues, causing spray drift, or broadly disturbing the soil like tillage. That said, biodiversity outcomes depend on how the technology is used. Electrical weeding is effective on contacted vegetation, so operators should protect desirable plants, flowering resources, field margins, habitat strips, and sensitive areas through targeted application, timing, and local validation.

## Detailed Answer

Biodiversity is not affected by one pathway only. It includes non-target plants, crop plants, pollinators, beneficial insects, birds, aquatic life, soil organisms, field margins, cover crops, and the wider habitat structure of a farm or managed landscape. Zasso's Electroherb technology changes the risk profile because it is a physical, contact-based, non-chemical method rather than a sprayed herbicide.

The main positive biodiversity argument is reduced chemical exposure. Zasso does not apply herbicide active ingredients or surfactants to the field, so it avoids residue, drift, runoff, volatilization, and post-treatment chemical exposure pathways associated with herbicide programs. This can be valuable near sensitive crops, public spaces, waterways, pollinator habitat, organic systems, and areas where chemical load reduction is a customer priority.

Zasso can also reduce some physical disturbance compared with cultivation or tillage. Because the treatment works through electrical contact with plants and the plant-soil circuit, it does not need to invert soil or mechanically cut through the seedbed in the way many tillage-based weed-control tools do. That can help protect soil structure, reduce erosion risk, and preserve some soil habitat functions in suitable use cases.

The important caveat is that vegetation itself is part of biodiversity. Weeds can be agronomic competitors, invasive species, disease hosts, or safety hazards, but some non-crop plants also provide pollen, nectar, shelter, alternative prey, nesting sites, or habitat connectivity. If electrical weeding removes flowering plants, field-margin vegetation, cover crops, or habitat features at the wrong time or in the wrong place, it can reduce resources for pollinators, beneficial insects, birds, or other organisms, even though no chemical residue is left behind.

For that reason, the safest customer-facing position is conditional. Zasso can be a biodiversity-compatible tool when it is used as part of a planned integrated weed management program, with clear target weeds, protected habitat areas, appropriate timing, trained operators, and site-specific protocols. It should not be presented as automatically improving biodiversity in every field or every pass.

## What This Means for Customers

For customers, Zasso is strongest where the goal is to reduce herbicide dependence while keeping weed control practical. This may matter in orchards, vineyards, organic production, public areas, infrastructure, sensitive sites, field edges, and operations working under sustainability, residue, or biodiversity expectations.

Customers should treat biodiversity as a management outcome, not only a machine attribute. A good plan identifies which vegetation should be controlled, which vegetation should be preserved, when pollinators or beneficial insects are active, where water bodies or protected habitats are located, and whether local regulations or certification rules require buffers or documentation.

In suitable applications, Zasso can help customers reduce chemical pressure, avoid spray drift, avoid herbicide residues, reduce soil disturbance compared with mechanical alternatives, and target unwanted vegetation more precisely. The best results come when the technology is combined with scouting, timing, habitat preservation, integrated weed management, and local performance monitoring.

## Safe Sales Wording

"Zasso can be part of a biodiversity-conscious weed management program because it controls vegetation without herbicide residues, spray drift, or broad soil disturbance. The biodiversity outcome depends on how the treatment is planned, timed, and targeted."

"The technology helps reduce chemical exposure pathways, but we still protect flowering resources, field margins, cover crops, and desirable plants where they are part of the site's biodiversity plan."

"In suitable applications, Zasso supports reduced-herbicide strategies while keeping weed control practical and targeted."

## Caveats

Do not claim that Zasso automatically improves biodiversity, has zero environmental impact, or is harmless to all organisms. Biodiversity outcomes depend on crop system, weed species, habitat layout, flowering stage, pollinator activity, soil conditions, treatment frequency, applicator design, operator skill, local regulations, and conservation objectives.

Do not describe electrical weeding as biologically selective. It can injure or kill desirable plants if they are contacted or included in the effective current path. Removing flowering weeds, cover crops, or field-margin vegetation can also reduce forage, shelter, or habitat connectivity if the treatment is poorly timed or poorly targeted.

Do not use soil-arthropod or microbial evidence as proof for every biodiversity endpoint. Soil organisms, pollinators, birds, aquatic life, non-target plants, and wider habitat effects each require appropriate evidence and local interpretation.
